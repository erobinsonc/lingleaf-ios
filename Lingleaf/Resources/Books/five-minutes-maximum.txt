Chapter 1: The Request

The message arrived on a Wednesday night in February, at 11:47. Thabo Nkosi was lying on the floor of his apartment in Cape Town with his headphones on, listening to a track he had been mixing for six hours and had begun, at some point in the last two, to actively hate.

It was from Jonah. That in itself was strange. They had not spoken properly in almost two years.

"Hey. I know this is out of nowhere. Nadia and I are getting married. 14 December, at her aunt's place in the winelands. I'd really like you to be my best man. No pressure. Well. Some pressure. Call me?"

Thabo read it three times. Then he took off his headphones, put his phone face down on the carpet, and he lay there for some time looking at the ceiling, where a long crack ran from the light fitting to the corner like a river on a map.

They had met when they were eleven, on the first day of high school, when Jonah Abrahams had been seated next to him in a math class and had spent the entire lesson drawing a remarkably accurate picture of the teacher as a tortoise. By fourteen, they had formed a band. By eighteen, the band had a name, a following of roughly forty people and a single song that had once been played on a student radio station at two in the morning. By twenty-four, the band had broken up, and so, more or less, had they.

The reasons were complicated, or Thabo told himself they were. Jonah had gone to London to do a master's in sound design and had stayed. Thabo had been supposed to go too. He had been accepted on the same program. He had had a plane ticket.

Then, two weeks before the flight, his mother had been sick: not seriously, as it turned out, but seriously enough at the time. He had stayed in Cape Town to help. Then he had kept on staying, long after there was any real reason to, and somewhere in the middle of all that staying he and Jonah had stopped calling each other.

There had been no argument. That was almost the worst part. There had just been a silence that grew longer and harder to break, until one day it seemed simpler not to try.

He picked up his phone again and looked at the message. "No pressure. Well. Some pressure." It was so exactly how Jonah talked that for a moment it was as if no time had passed at all.

He typed: "Of course. I'd be honored." He looked at it. It sounded like a letter from a bank. He deleted "honored" and wrote "I'd love to." Then he deleted everything and wrote: "You absolute idiot. Yes."

He pressed send before he could think about it, and then he lay back down on the floor and put his headphones on again. He realized that he no longer hated the track quite so much.

Chapter 2: The Rules

Jonah called the next evening. His voice was the same, though there was a slight new roundness in his vowels, the faint mark of two years in London, which Thabo noticed and decided not to mention.

They talked for an hour and a half. About Nadia, mostly, whom Jonah had met in London at a friend's birthday dinner. She was a pediatric nurse from a family in the northern suburbs of Cape Town, and she had apparently told him on their second date that if he ever wanted to be taken seriously, he would need to stop wearing that hat. About the wedding, which was going to be small, by Cape Town standards, meaning only about a hundred and forty people. About Jonah's job, recording sound for documentaries, which took him to places like Iceland and Mozambique and involved, he said, a great deal of standing very still in fields at dawn holding a microphone and trying not to breathe.

About the last two years, they talked hardly at all. Both of them, Thabo noticed, steered carefully around it, as though it were a hole in the road.

"There's one thing," Jonah said at the end. "Nadia has a rule about the speeches."

"A rule?"

"Several rules, actually. She's very organized. She sent me a document."

There was a rustle of paper. "I'll read it to you. 'Speeches. One: the best man's speech must not exceed five minutes. Two: it is essential that it contain no stories involving former girlfriends, police officers or any of the events of the year 2016. Three: the speaker must not mention the band.'"

"I can't mention the band?"

"Not even once. She says she's been to too many weddings where the best man spends the whole speech talking about himself."

"The band was half your life."

"I know," said Jonah. "That's sort of her point."

Thabo was silent for a moment. "What were the events of 2016?"

"Exactly," said Jonah. "That's the spirit." He paused. "Seriously, though. Thanks for saying yes. I wasn't sure you would."

"Neither was I," said Thabo, before he could stop himself.

There was a pause on the line, long enough to be noticed, short enough to be forgiven.

"Well," said Jonah. "You did. Five minutes, maximum. I'll see you in December."

After the call, Thabo did something he had not done in a long time. He went to the shelf above his desk, where a row of old hard drives sat gathering dust, and he found the one labeled, in his own sixteen-year-old handwriting, BAND — DO NOT DELETE. It took him twenty minutes to find a cable that fit it.

The song was still there. The one that had been played on student radio at two in the morning. He put on his headphones and listened to it from beginning to end, sitting very still in the dark. It was not, objectively, a good song. The drums were too loud, the lyrics were the lyrics of two boys who had never been in love, and there was a moment in the second verse where Jonah's guitar went audibly out of tune. But underneath all of that there was something he had not heard in his own music for years: two people who were not yet afraid of anything, playing as though nobody would ever tell them to stop.

He listened to it three times. Then he unplugged the drive and put it back on the shelf, very carefully, as though it might break.

Chapter 3: Draft One

He began the speech the following Saturday morning, at his kitchen table, with a cup of coffee and an enormous feeling of confidence that lasted approximately nine minutes.

Writing, he discovered, was not like mixing music. When a track was wrong, he could hear it at once. He could isolate the bass, or the vocals, or the faint hum in the left channel, and fix it.

A speech was different. Every sentence sounded fine in isolation and terrible in combination. Every joke was either too old or too obscure. Every sincere remark sounded, when he read it back, like the inside of a greeting card.

It did not help that he had other things to do. Thabo worked, officially, as a sound engineer at a small studio in Woodstock, recording advertisements for radio, voice-overs for corporate videos and, now and then, a band that could not afford anywhere better. Unofficially, he worked on his own music at night, in the apartment, alone, tracks that he finished and never released and occasionally deleted. He had been doing this for two years. He had told himself it was a period of development. He had begun, recently, to suspect that it was a period of hiding.

By lunchtime, he had a draft. It was, he thought, quite good. It began with a joke about how Jonah had once tried to impress a girl by playing the guitar on a bus and had been asked to leave at the next stop. It continued with a series of affectionate stories about their schooldays, the tortoise drawing among them. It contained a clever pun on the word "ring", as in the wedding ring and as in the fact that Jonah never rang anyone back. It ended with a warm tribute to Nadia, whom he had never met.

He timed it with the stopwatch on his phone. Nine minutes and forty seconds.

He read it again. Then he read it a third time, out loud, to the empty kitchen, and somewhere around the fourth minute he realized that the speech had a problem much larger than its length.

It was about two people he no longer was. The Jonah in the speech was fifteen. The Thabo in the speech was his loyal, funny sidekick. They were both, in the speech, exactly the people they had been before London, before the plane ticket, before the silence. Spoken aloud in a garden full of a hundred and forty strangers, it would sound charming. It would also be, in a way that only the two of them would understand, a lie.

He deleted it. Then, panicking slightly, he pressed undo and saved a copy in a folder called "bad ideas", which already contained several unfinished songs and a half-written letter to a university he had never sent.

Chapter 4: Advice

Over the next few weeks, he asked everyone he knew for advice, and everyone gave it.

His sister, Lindiwe, who was a lawyer and gave advice the way some people breathe, told him that the secret was structure. "Open with a hook, three short stories, one emotional moment, then thank the parents and finish," she said. "Like a closing argument. Nobody cares what you say as long as you sit down on time."

His roommate, Carlos, who was from Maputo and played the trumpet in a jazz band on weekends, told him that the secret was spontaneity. "Don't write anything," he said. "Just stand up and talk. The truth comes out when you're nervous." This seemed to Thabo like excellent advice for a jazz musician and catastrophic advice for anyone else.

"You don't trust yourself," Carlos added, when Thabo said so. He was cleaning his trumpet at the kitchen table, polishing each valve as carefully as if it were jewelry. "That's your problem with the music too. You make something good, and then you sit on it for six months, and then you decide it isn't good enough, and nobody ever hears it." He held the trumpet up to the light. "Some things only work if you let other people hear them before they're perfect."

"Thank you, Carlos."

"You're welcome," said Carlos, who was impervious to sarcasm in any of his four languages.

His mother, who was entirely recovered now and had taken up hiking with a group of retired teachers, told him nothing at all at first. She simply listened while he explained the problem over Sunday lunch, and nodded, and passed him the potatoes.

"So," she said, when he had finished. "The speech isn't the difficulty."

"The speech is the whole difficulty. I have to write it by December."

"No," said his mother. "The speech is a speech. You could write a perfectly nice one in an hour, and everyone would clap, and nobody would remember it. That is what most best men do." She put down the serving spoon. "The difficulty is that you haven't spoken to Jonah properly in two years, and you want the speech to do it for you."

Thabo looked at his plate.

"It won't," his mother went on, more gently. "A speech is a terrible place for an apology. Everybody's watching, and nobody can answer back." She paused. "And, my boy, I think it's time you stopped blaming me for London."

He looked up sharply. "I've never blamed you."

"No. You've never said it." She held his gaze. "But I was out of hospital in five days. You were the one who stayed for two years. I was very glad of your company. But I have wondered, more than once, whether I was your reason or your excuse."

It was, he thought afterward, the most painful thing anyone had said to him in a long time. It was also, he suspected, true. Nor could he think of a single argument against it, though he tried, all the way home on the train, and for most of the night.

At some point after midnight he opened his messages and began to type to Jonah. "I need to tell you something about London." He looked at it for a long time. Then he added: "It wasn't Mum. It was me." The cursor blinked at the end of the line, patient and merciless.

He did not send it. A message at one in the morning, he thought, was not much braver than a speech in a garden. It was another way of saying something without having to hear the answer. If he was going to say it, he would have to say it with his voice, to a person who could reply.

He deleted the message, one letter at a time, and went to bed, and did not sleep.

Chapter 5: Draft Seven

By November, there were seven drafts in the folder called "bad ideas".

Draft two had been funny and empty. Draft three had been sincere and unbearable. Draft four had followed Lindiwe's structure so closely that it read like the summary of a court case. Draft five had attempted spontaneity, as Carlos recommended, and consisted of a single sheet of paper bearing the words "JUST TALK". Draft six had been written at two in the morning and had contained the sentence "friendship is like a song that changes key", which Thabo had later deleted with a shudder.

Draft seven was the one he kept returning to. He had written it on a Sunday night after the conversation with his mother, quickly, almost without stopping, the way he used to write songs at seventeen. When he read it back the next morning, he had expected to wince. He hadn't.

It was short. It was not, strictly speaking, funny. And it broke one of Nadia's rules.

He did not know what to do about that.

In the end, he did something he had not done in two years. He called Jonah first.

It was evening in London, and Jonah answered from what sounded like the inside of a wind tunnel. "Sorry," he shouted. "I'm on a cliff. Recording seabirds. Hang on." There was a great deal of noise, and then a sudden quiet, as though a door had closed. "Okay. I'm in the car. What's up? Is it the speech? Is it nine minutes? Lindiwe told Nadia's cousin it was nine minutes."

"It's not nine minutes," said Thabo. "It's four. But I need to ask you something first."

"Go on."

Thabo took a breath. "I don't think I can write it without talking about the band."

There was a silence on the line.

"Not the band exactly," Thabo said. "Not stories, not the song on the radio, none of that. But London. The plane ticket. The fact that I didn't come." He closed his eyes. "Every version I write without it feels like I'm standing up and pretending. And I've done enough of that in the last two years to last me a while."

The silence went on for so long that Thabo began to wonder whether the call had dropped.

"I was so angry with you," Jonah said at last, very quietly. "Do you know that? Not because you stayed. Your mum was ill. Of course you stayed. Anyone would have. I was angry because you never told me why you kept on staying. You just stopped answering. I thought I'd done something. I spent about a year trying to work out what."

"You hadn't done anything," said Thabo. "I was scared. Of London, of the program, of being worse than you at the thing we'd both said we were going to do. Mum getting ill was a reason to stay for two weeks. After that it was an excuse." He swallowed. "My mother told me that, actually. Last month. Over potatoes."

To his astonishment, Jonah laughed: a short, startled laugh, half relief and half something else.

"Your mother," he said, "has always been the smartest person in any room she walks into."

"She'd agree with you."

"She would." Jonah paused. "Okay. Here's what I think. I think you should write the speech you need to write. And I'll deal with Nadia."

"She has a document."

"She does," said Jonah. "But she also has a heart, which the document doesn't mention." There was a rustle, as of a seat belt. "Just... promise me one thing."

"What?"

"Keep it under five minutes. Otherwise she'll actually kill both of us."

When the call ended, Thabo sat for a long time with the phone in his hand. Then he went into the living room, where Carlos was watching a soccer game with the sound off and a trumpet across his knees, and he took a deep breath.

"Can I read you something?" he asked. "It's four minutes. You don't have to say anything."

Carlos turned off the television without a word.

Thabo read him draft seven. His voice shook on the second page and steadied on the third. When he finished, Carlos was quiet for a while, turning the trumpet over in his hands.

"You wrote that down," Carlos said at last.

"Yes."

"I told you not to write anything."

"I know."

"I was wrong," said Carlos, and turned the television back on. Coming from Carlos, it was the highest praise Thabo had ever received.

Chapter 6: The Winelands

The wedding was held in a garden at the foot of a mountain, an hour's drive outside the city, on a December afternoon so clear and warm that the mountain looked as if someone had painted it onto the sky. Long tables had been set out under old oak trees. Strings of small lights had been hung between the branches, waiting for the evening. Beyond the garden, rows of vines ran away toward the hills in long, green, orderly lines.

Lindiwe drove him out from the city that morning, because, she said, she did not trust him to arrive on time, in one piece, with the rings. The road climbed out of the suburbs and through a pass in the mountains, and for a long time neither of them said a word. The radio played quietly. Somewhere near the top of the pass, a song came on that both of them recognized, an old one from their parents' generation, and Lindiwe turned it up without asking.

"Mum told me what she said to you," she remarked, when it had finished. "About London."

Thabo looked out of the window. "Of course she did."

"She felt terrible about it afterward. She thought she'd been too harsh."

"She wasn't," said Thabo. "She was right. That's worse."

His sister glanced at him, then back at the road. "For what it's worth," she said, "I always thought you'd go eventually. Not to London, necessarily. Just... somewhere. Out of that apartment. You've been living as though you were waiting for permission." She paused. "Nobody's going to give it to you, you know. Not even a bride."

"Is that legal advice?"

"That's free advice," said Lindiwe. "Legal advice costs extra."

Thabo arrived early, wearing a borrowed suit that was slightly too tight across the shoulders, with the speech folded into four in his inside pocket. He had met Nadia the night before, at a small dinner for the two families. She was tall and quick and direct, with a laugh that seemed to surprise even her. Within ten minutes of being introduced, she had taken him aside on the restaurant balcony.

"Jonah tells me you want to break rule three," she had said.

"I'm sorry," Thabo had said. "I can explain..."

"He's explained." She had studied him for a long moment, with exactly the kind of look, he imagined, that she gave to small patients who claimed they had not eaten any of the cookies. "Here's my position. Rule three exists because I didn't want a speech about two boys in a band. If what you're going to say is about two men who stopped speaking and started again, that isn't a band story." She had smiled suddenly. "It's a wedding story. Just keep it under five minutes."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because everyone has met you," Nadia had said, and gone back inside.

Jonah had been at the dinner too, of course. It was the first time they had been in the same room in two years. Thabo had rehearsed the moment so many times in his head, in so many versions, that when it finally came it was almost disappointing in its simplicity. Jonah had seen him from across the restaurant, stood up, walked straight over and hugged him, hard, for a long time, without saying anything at all. He smelled of the same cheap shampoo he had used at fifteen.

"You've cut your hair," Jonah had said eventually, stepping back.

"You've stopped wearing that hat."

"Nadia," said Jonah, by way of explanation, and both of them had laughed, too loudly, the way people laugh when the alternative is crying in a restaurant. Then Jonah's grandmother had demanded that Thabo come and sit beside her and explain why he was so thin, and the evening had carried them along, and there had been no time for anything else. It did not seem to matter. Some conversations, Thabo thought, had already happened, on a phone in a car on a cliff; what remained was simply to be in the same place again.

Now, in the garden, the ceremony came and went in a blur of music, sunlight and other people's tears. Thabo stood at Jonah's shoulder and held the rings, and did not drop them, and managed not to cry until the very end. That was when Jonah's grandmother, who was ninety-one and very small, stood up in the front row and said "At last!" in a voice that carried across the entire garden.

Then came the long, golden part of the afternoon: the photographs, the food, the children running between the tables, the slow lengthening of the shadows across the grass. Thabo ate almost nothing. He kept touching the speech in his pocket, as if it might have escaped.

Just before seven, as the lights came on in the oak trees, Lindiwe appeared at his side.

"You're on after the bride's father," she said. "How long is it?"

"Four minutes twenty."

His sister looked at him with an expression he could not quite interpret. "Is it good?"

"I don't know," said Thabo. "It's true. I'm hoping that counts."

Chapter 7: Four Minutes Twenty

The bride's father talked for eleven minutes, which nobody seemed to mind, and included a long story about Nadia at the age of four refusing to leave a shoe shop, which was greeted with tremendous applause. Then the master of ceremonies, a cousin with a microphone and a great deal of confidence, announced the best man.

Thabo stood up. The garden was very quiet. A hundred and forty faces turned toward him in the soft light, and somewhere at the back a child dropped a spoon. He unfolded the paper. His hands, he noticed, were completely steady, which surprised him more than anything had all day.

"Good evening," he said. "I'm Thabo. I've known Jonah since we were eleven, when he was seated next to me in a math class and spent the entire lesson drawing the teacher as a tortoise."

There was laughter, warm and easy. He let it settle.

"I've been told," he went on, "that I have five minutes, maximum. I've also been told that I may not mention former girlfriends, police officers or the year 2016." More laughter. "I'm going to keep all of those rules. I'm going to break one other, with the bride's permission, which I have, and which I'm told is the only permission that matters from now on."

He glanced at Nadia. She raised her eyebrows at him, and then, very slightly, nodded.

"Two years ago," Thabo said, "Jonah and I were supposed to go to London together. He went. I didn't. I had a good reason for not going, for about two weeks. After that, I had a series of bad reasons, and then I had no reasons at all, only a silence that I let grow until it seemed too big to cross. That was my fault, not his. I want to say that here, in front of all of you, not because a wedding is the right place for an apology, because it really isn't..."

He paused. Several people laughed, gently, and he was grateful to them.

"...but because I want you all to know something about the man Nadia has just married. Three months ago, after two years of silence, he sent me a message asking me to stand next to him today. He didn't ask me to explain first. He didn't demand that I apologize, though he had every right to. He just asked. And when I finally did explain, on the phone, while he was sitting in a car on a cliff in England covered in seabird feathers, the first thing he did was laugh. And the second thing he did was forgive me."

The garden was entirely silent now. Jonah was looking down at the table. Nadia had put her hand over his.

"So that is who Jonah is," Thabo said. "Not the best guitarist I know, although he is. Not the person who drew the best tortoise in the history of our school, although he did. He's the person who keeps the door open. Long after anyone else would have closed it. Long after I gave him any reason to keep it open."

He turned to Nadia. "Nadia, I've known you for exactly twenty-two hours. In that time you've given me rules, a deadline and a lecture about cookies, and I've liked you more with every one of them. He is lucky. He knows he is. I suspect you remind him regularly."

Laughter again, and Nadia laughing loudest of all.

"There's a word in English," said Thabo, "that I spent a long time thinking about while I was writing this. 'Best man.' As if there were a competition and I'd won it. I didn't. I'm not the best man here today. Nowhere near." He lifted his glass of ginger beer toward the head table. "But I'm very glad to be the man standing next to the best one. To Jonah and Nadia."

"To Jonah and Nadia," said a hundred and forty voices, and the garden broke into applause.

Thabo sat down. Under the table, his knees had begun, finally, to shake. Lindiwe leaned over from the next seat and pressed the stopwatch on her phone into his hand. It said 4:18.

"Two seconds early," she said. "Not bad, for a man with no structure."

Across the garden, his mother caught his eye from the table where she was sitting with Jonah's grandmother. She did not wave or smile. She simply put her hand flat on her heart for a moment, and then went back to her conversation, as if nothing had happened. It was, Thabo thought, exactly the kind of review he had always wanted for his music and had never been brave enough to ask for: brief, private and impossible to argue with.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. It was a message from Carlos, who had not been invited and had demanded news as it happened. "Well?" it said.

"Four minutes eighteen," Thabo typed. "Nobody threw anything."

"Told you," came the reply. "Truth comes out when you're nervous." Thabo decided, on reflection, to let him have that one.

Later, much later, when the dancing had begun and the lights in the oak trees were the only ones still burning, Jonah found him at the edge of the garden, looking out at the dark rows of vines.

For a while, neither of them said anything. They did not need to. Somewhere behind them, the band was playing a song they had both loved at sixteen, and the guests were singing along, badly.

"You know they asked me to play something," Jonah said eventually. "Nadia's cousin. He has a guitar in his car. He wants the groom to play for the bride."

"Are you going to?"

"Only if you play with me," said Jonah. "It's been two years. I'll need someone to cover my mistakes."

Thabo looked at him. "Rule three," he said.

"Rule three is about speeches," said Jonah. "Nadia was very precise. I checked."

"Of course you did."

"Of course I did." Jonah held out his hand. "Five minutes, maximum. Come on."

Thabo had once been too afraid to get on a plane, and then too proud to pick up a phone, and then too ashamed to do either for two long years. Now he took his oldest friend's hand. Together they walked back toward the music and the light in the trees, toward a hundred and forty strangers who were about to hear the two of them play together for the first time since they were boys.

It ran, in the end, to seven minutes. Nobody complained. Least of all the bride.

The following Monday, back in the apartment, Thabo sat down at his desk at nine in the morning instead of midnight. He opened the folder where his finished tracks lived, the ones he had never let anyone hear, and he chose four. He did not listen to them again first. He knew that if he listened to them again, he would find something wrong, and if he found something wrong, he would wait another six months.

He wrote a short email to a small label in Johannesburg that Carlos had mentioned once. Then he wrote another to the program in London that had accepted him two years earlier. He asked, as politely as he could, whether it accepted late applications from people who had lost their nerve. He attached the tracks. His finger hovered over the button for a moment.

Then he sent both emails, closed the laptop and went into the kitchen, where Carlos was making coffee.

"You look strange," said Carlos.

"I've just sent some music to people."

Carlos considered this. Then he poured a second cup, and handed it to him, and raised his own in a small, solemn toast.

"Before it was perfect?" he asked.

"Before it was perfect," said Thabo, and drank.
