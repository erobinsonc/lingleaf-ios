Chapter 1: The Biggest Meeting of the Year

Wanjiru had worked at Jioni Solar for three years, and she had never been this nervous about a meeting. The company was small, only twenty-two people in an office above a pharmacy in Nairobi. It designed and sold solar lamps and small solar panels for homes that were not connected to the electricity network.

On Monday morning, her manager, Mr. Otieno, called her into his office. He was a calm, gray-haired man who always kept a bowl of roasted peanuts on his desk for visitors.

"The Lake Region Farmers' Cooperative has agreed to hear our proposal," he said. "Four hundred families. If they choose us, it will be the biggest contract we have ever had."

Wanjiru sat up straight. She had been trying to get a meeting with the cooperative for almost a year.

"The meeting is on Thursday, in Kisumu," he continued. "I would go myself, but my daughter is getting married on Saturday. My wife has told me that if I leave Nairobi this week, I should not come back." He smiled. "So it's yours. You know the product better than anyone."

Wanjiru spent the next two days preparing. She built a presentation with forty slides, full of charts, photographs and technical details. She calculated exactly how much each family would save on kerosene over five years. She practiced in front of the mirror, in front of her sister and, once, in front of her neighbor's very patient cat.

On Wednesday evening, she took the night bus to Kisumu with her laptop, a box of sample lamps and a small suitcase. She slept badly and dreamed about charts.

Chapter 2: A Small Disaster

The bus arrived in Kisumu at six in the morning. The air was warm and smelled of the lake. Wanjiru checked into a small hotel near the center and had a shower and a cup of strong tea. Then she opened her laptop to go through her slides one last time.

The screen stayed black.

She pressed the power button again. Nothing. She plugged in the charger, waited, and tried again. Still nothing. She turned the laptop upside down, as if the problem might simply fall out of it.

At the hotel reception, a young man called Brian looked at it kindly. He said that it might be the battery, or it might be something more serious. He told her there was a repair shop on the next street, but that it didn't open until ten.

The meeting was at nine.

Wanjiru went back to her room and sat on the bed. She felt sick. Everything was on that laptop. She had saved the presentation on the office computer too, but the office computer was four hundred kilometers away.

She called her colleague Moraa in Nairobi. Moraa listened, then said that she would email the file immediately. Wanjiru could print it, she suggested, or show it on someone's phone.

"Forty slides on a phone?" said Wanjiru. "They'll fall asleep."

"Then don't use forty slides," said Moraa. "You know this product, Wanjiru. You could explain it with your eyes closed."

"If I could explain it with my eyes closed," said Wanjiru, "I wouldn't have made forty slides."

But after she hung up, she kept thinking about what Moraa had said.

She took the hotel notepad from the desk and a pen with the hotel's name on it. At the top of the first page, she wrote: "What do they need?" Then she sat still for a long time.

If she were a farmer near the lake, what would she want to know? Not the size of the battery, she decided. Not the angle of the panel. She would want to know whether the lamp would last and whether it would save her money. And she would want someone to answer the phone if it broke.

She wrote those three questions down and drew a box around them. It wasn't much of a plan. It was the opposite of forty slides. But it was all she had, and the meeting was in two hours.

Chapter 3: The Cooperative

The cooperative's office was a long, low building on the edge of the city, with a metal roof and a garden full of banana plants. Inside, about fifteen people were sitting on plastic chairs in a meeting room. Most of them were farmers. Some had clearly come straight from their fields, and a few were holding notebooks.

At the front sat the chairwoman, Mrs. Akinyi. She was a small, serious woman in her sixties, with reading glasses on a chain around her neck. She shook Wanjiru's hand firmly.

"We have heard three presentations this month," she said. "All three companies told us that their lamps were the best in the world. All three of them showed us many charts. Our members are tired of charts."

Wanjiru thought about her dead laptop. For the first time that morning, she nearly laughed.

"Then you're lucky," she said. "I don't have any charts today."

She explained what had happened. Some of the farmers smiled. One older man said that his own computer had died the week before. He had decided that it must have been broken by his grandson, who had been playing games on it.

"So, no charts," said Mrs. Akinyi. "What do you have?"

Wanjiru looked at the box at her feet. "I have lamps," she said. "And I have questions."

Chapter 4: Questions Instead of Slides

She opened the box and passed the lamps around the room. They were simple. Each one had a yellow plastic body and a small panel that charged in the sun. A handle meant that it could be hung from the ceiling. She asked people to turn them on and off, to feel the weight, to look at the panel.

Then, instead of talking about the product, she asked them about their evenings.

"What happens in your house after it gets dark?" she asked.

At first, nobody answered. Then a young woman at the back raised her hand. Her name was Awino. She said that her children did their homework by the light of a kerosene lamp. The smoke made them cough, she said, and the smell stayed in their clothes.

An older man said that he bought kerosene twice a week, and that the price had gone up again last month. Another man explained that he charged his phone at a shop in the market, which cost him money and took an hour of walking. A woman near the window said that she had burned her hand on a kerosene lamp the year before. She still had the mark.

Wanjiru listened. She wrote everything down on a big sheet of paper that she had borrowed from the office manager. She didn't interrupt.

She had never let a meeting run like this before, without a plan, and it terrified her a little. But the room had changed. People were leaning forward. They were talking to each other, not just to her.

"Can I ask you something?" she said, when they had finished. "If you had a lamp like this one, what would you do differently in the evenings?"

The answers came quickly now. Awino said that her children would study longer, and that she might start sewing again at night, to sell clothes at the market. The older man said that he would save the kerosene money for school fees. Someone said that if he had light outside, he would feel safer walking outside to the bathroom at night. Everyone laughed, but they also nodded.

Wanjiru wrote it all down. Then she turned the paper around so that everyone could see it.

"I came here with forty slides," she said. "They said the same things that you have just told me. But you said them better."

Chapter 5: The Difficult Question

Not everyone was convinced. A tall man in a blue shirt, who had been silent until now, crossed his arms.

"These lamps look nice," he said. "But the last company that came here sold lamps to my cousin's village. After eight months, half of them were broken. The company said that the farmers must have used them wrongly. Then they stopped answering the phone."

The room went quiet. Wanjiru knew this was the most important moment of the meeting. If she gave a weak answer now, everything else would be lost.

"I can't speak for that company," she said slowly. "Maybe the lamps were badly made. Maybe the company should have trained people better. Or maybe they simply didn't care after they had been paid. I don't know."

She picked up a lamp. "What I can tell you is what we do. Every lamp has a two-year guarantee. If it breaks, we replace it. We don't argue about whose fault it was. And we train one person in every village to do small repairs, so you don't have to wait for someone from Nairobi."

"Anyone can promise that," said the man.

"You're right," said Wanjiru. "So don't believe me. Call our customers." She took out her phone. "We have worked in two villages near Kakamega for eighteen months. I can give you the phone number of the woman who does repairs there. Her name is Mama Esther. She will tell you if we have kept our promises. And if we haven't, she will definitely tell you that too."

To her surprise, Mrs. Akinyi laughed. "Give me the number," she said. "I'll call her this afternoon."

Chapter 6: The Long Wait

After the meeting, Wanjiru took her laptop to the repair shop. The technician, a cheerful man with a radio playing behind him, opened it in five minutes. He told her that the battery had died, probably from the heat on the bus. If she had kept it in her bag instead of under the window, he said, it might have survived.

"Could I have done anything else?" she asked.

"You could have bought a better laptop," he said, and laughed at his own joke. He replaced the battery, and the screen came back to life. There were her forty slides, perfect and useless.

That evening, she called Mr. Otieno and told him what had happened. She expected him to be disappointed. Instead, he was quiet for a long moment.

"You didn't show them any slides at all?" he asked.

"Not one."

"Hm," he said. "You must have been very nervous."

"I was terrified," she admitted. "I should have saved the file on my phone. I should have brought a printed copy. I made a lot of mistakes."

"Maybe," said Mr. Otieno. "But you might have made a bigger mistake if your laptop had worked." He paused. "Let's wait and see what they say."

Wanjiru took the night bus back to Nairobi. She didn't sleep much. She kept replaying the meeting in her head. She thought about the tall man with the crossed arms and wondered what Mama Esther would say about them.

Chapter 7: The Phone Call

For the first four days, there was no news. Wanjiru went back to her normal work: answering emails, visiting a shop in Thika, fixing a problem with a delivery to Nakuru. Every time her phone rang, her heart jumped. Every time, it was someone else.

On the fifth day, it was Mama Esther. She was calling from Kakamega, and she sounded amused.

She said that a woman called Mrs. Akinyi had phoned her and had asked her a great many questions. How often did the lamps break? How long did repairs take? Had the company ever refused to replace one? Did people in the village still use kerosene?

"She asked me everything except my shoe size," said Mama Esther. "I told her the truth. I hope that was all right."

"That's exactly what I wanted," said Wanjiru. "What did she say at the end?"

"She said that she would talk to the members," Mama Esther replied. "Then she asked me if I had ever thought about moving to Kisumu. I said no. Kakamega is my home."

On the ninth day, as she was eating lunch at her desk, her phone showed a number from Kisumu. It was Mrs. Akinyi.

The chairwoman told her that the members had voted the evening before. She said that she had called Mama Esther, as promised, and that they had talked for almost an hour. Mama Esther had told her that two lamps in her village had broken in eighteen months. Both of them had been replaced within a week.

"She also told me that your company sent her a birthday message last year," said Mrs. Akinyi. "She said that no company had ever done that for her before."

Wanjiru held her breath.

"The members voted for Jioni Solar," Mrs. Akinyi said. "Four hundred families, if the price is what you said. The tall man, Joseph, voted for you too. He said that if you had shown him charts, he would have voted against you. But you asked questions. He liked that."

Wanjiru thanked her three times. When she put down the phone, she realized that the whole office was looking at her. Moraa was standing in the doorway with both hands over her mouth.

"Well?" said Moraa.

"We got it," said Wanjiru.

The office exploded in noise. Someone turned up the music. Mr. Otieno came out of his office, looked at everyone celebrating, and quietly put a fresh bowl of peanuts on Wanjiru's desk.

Chapter 8: The New Presentation

A month later, the first two hundred lamps were delivered to the cooperative. Wanjiru traveled to Kisumu again to train the village repair workers. This time she took the day bus, and she kept her laptop in her bag, far away from the window.

The training took place in the garden of the cooperative, under a large mango tree, because the meeting room was too hot in the afternoon. Wanjiru showed the group how to open a lamp, how to test the battery and how to replace a broken switch. Most of the parts were held together with simple screws, and that had been a deliberate choice. If a lamp could only be repaired in a factory, she explained, it would not be repaired at all.

Awino, the young woman from the meeting, was one of the people who had been chosen for the training. She learned fast and asked good questions. On the last day, she told Wanjiru that she had already started sewing at night. She had sold four dresses at the market that week.

Back in Nairobi, Mr. Otieno asked Wanjiru to prepare a short talk for the whole team about what she had learned. She agreed, and then she sat at her desk for a long time, looking at a blank screen.

In the end, she made a presentation with only three slides.

The first one said: "Ask about their evenings."

The second one said: "Listen more than you talk."

And the third one, in big letters, said: "Keep your laptop away from the bus window."

Everyone laughed at the last one. But afterward, two of her colleagues told her that they had changed their own presentations. One of them, a young salesman called Kevin, said that he had deleted twenty-five slides. He said that if he had heard her story a year earlier, he would have saved himself a lot of difficult meetings.

Wanjiru smiled. She still had her forty slides, saved on three different devices now. She hadn't opened them since Kisumu. She didn't think she would ever need them again.
