Part 1

Amir was sixteen, and he wanted a summer job. He lived in Toronto with his parents and his little brother. Most of his friends worked in cafes or shops at the mall. But Amir loved bikes. So in June, he walked into a bike shop on his street.

The shop belonged to a woman called Ms. Ortega. She was older than his mother. She had black oil on her hands.

"Can you fix a flat tire?" she asked.

"Yes," said Amir. "I fix my own bike all the time."

"Good. You can start on Monday. Nine to three."

The first week was harder than he expected. He cleaned the floor and washed old chains. He fixed tires and checked brakes. Ms. Ortega knew everything about bikes. She was patient, and she explained things clearly.

On Thursday, an old man came in with a small blue bike. It was very old, and the paint was coming off.

"This was my bike when I was a boy," he said. "My granddaughter, Lila, is seven on Saturday. I want to give it to her. Can you make it work again?"

Ms. Ortega looked at the bike. "We can try," she said. "Amir, this is your project."

Amir was excited, but he was also nervous. This bike was older than his father.

He worked on it all Thursday afternoon. He cleaned the chain, and he put on new tires. He painted it a brighter blue. By Friday morning, it looked almost new.

Part 2

Then Amir noticed a problem. A small part in the back wheel was broken. Without it, the wheel didn't turn.

"We don't have that part," said Ms. Ortega. "Nobody makes it now. I'm sorry, Amir."

Amir didn't want to stop. He looked at the bike for a long time. He thought about the old man and about Lila. Then he had an idea.

"My uncle has a shop across the city," he said. "He keeps parts from old bikes. Can I call him?"

His uncle Farid answered the phone. "Send me a photo," he said. Amir sent it, and ten minutes later, his uncle called back.

"I have one," said Uncle Farid. "But I close at five."

It was already four o'clock. The bus was too slow, because the traffic was bad on Fridays. So Amir took his own bike and rode across the city. He rode very fast. He arrived at four fifty-five, red and tired.

Uncle Farid laughed. "You're faster than a taxi," he said. He gave Amir a little box with the part inside.

Amir rode back to the shop. Ms. Ortega was waiting for him at the door. She looked at the little box and smiled. Together, they put the part in the wheel. Amir turned the pedal, and the wheel moved easily. It was very quiet.

On Saturday morning, the old man came back with a little girl. She had a big smile and no front teeth.

"Lila, this was my bike," he said. "Now it's yours."

Lila got on the bike and rode slowly around the shop. Then she rode outside and down the street. Her grandfather walked behind her. He was laughing.

Before he went home, the old man shook Amir's hand. "Thank you," he said. "You gave my old bike a new life."

That evening, Amir told his family about his week. His little brother listened with big eyes.

"Is it the best job in the world?" he asked.

Amir thought about it. His hands were dirty, and his legs were tired. He earned less money than his friends at the mall.

"Yes," he said. "It's the best."
