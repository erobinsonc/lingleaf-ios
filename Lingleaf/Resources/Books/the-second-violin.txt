Chapter 1: An Announcement Over Dumplings

The announcement came, as the important ones in that house usually did, at dinner, and on a Tuesday, which was dumpling night.

Marek had made the dumplings. He made them every Tuesday, a habit he had brought with him from his grandmother's kitchen in Kraków to a narrow weatherboard house in an inner suburb of Melbourne. He made them with enormous seriousness and very little skill, so that every Tuesday the kitchen looked as though a small flour bomb had gone off in it. Farah had long ago stopped commenting on the mess. The dumplings, after all, were delicious, and the children adored them, and there are worse things in a marriage than flour on the ceiling.

Kuba, who was five, was eating his dumplings in the correct order, which that week meant from largest to smallest. Leila, who was ten, was not eating hers at all. She was pushing one slowly around her plate with her fork, and she had the look of someone who had rehearsed a sentence many times and was now waiting for the right moment to say it.

Farah noticed. Of course she noticed. She was a nurse in a children's hospital; she had spent fifteen years reading the faces of children who were trying to work out how to say something difficult. She did not say anything, though. Experience had taught her that the quickest way to stop a child from speaking was to ask them what was wrong.

"I want to stop violin," Leila said.

Kuba stopped chewing. Marek, who had been reaching for the sour cream, froze with his hand in the air, like a man caught in a photograph.

"Stop," Farah repeated carefully. "You mean take a break?"

"I mean stop. Forever." Leila put down her fork. "I've thought about it a lot. I'm not just saying it because I'm tired. I know the recital is a week from Saturday. I'll play in it, because I promised Ms. Adeyemi, but after that I don't want to have lessons anymore."

It was, Farah would think later, an extremely well-organized speech. It had a beginning, a middle and an end. It had anticipated her first objection and answered it in advance. It was the speech of a child who had been preparing for a fight.

"Can we talk about this after dinner?" she said.

"We can talk about it now," said Leila. "Nothing's going to change."

"After dinner," said Marek, gently but firmly, and passed his daughter the sour cream, which she had not asked for. It was the kind of small, absurd gesture he was very good at. Leila looked at the sour cream, and then at her father, and something in her face relaxed, very slightly.

Kuba, who had been following all of this with great interest, chose this moment to announce that he, too, would like to stop violin.

"You don't play violin," said Leila.

"Then I'll stop something else," said Kuba, and went back to his dumplings.

Chapter 2: Things That Are Not Said

The violin was not just a violin. Everyone in the house knew this, except perhaps Kuba, and nobody ever said it.

It had belonged to Farah's mother, Mahin, who had played second violin in a professional orchestra in Tehran for eleven years before she married, emigrated and, somewhere between one country and the next, simply stopped. Mahin had never explained why. She had brought the violin with her to Australia in its battered black case. For twenty years it had lived on top of a closet, and Farah, as a girl, used to look up at it the way other children look up at a locked attic.

When Farah was eight, her mother had taken it down. Lessons had begun the following week. Practice was every day, before school, for forty-five minutes, without exception. Mahin did not shout, and she did not punish. She simply sat in the kitchen with her tea while Farah practiced in the next room, and she listened.

From time to time she called out a single word in Persian, like a correction written in the margin of a page. Farah could still hear some of those words. "Slower. Again. Breathe."

At fifteen, Farah had stopped. There had been no announcement over dumplings.

What there had been, she could still remember with uncomfortable clarity. A Sunday in winter. A competition three weeks away, for which Mahin had insisted that she prepare a piece far too difficult for her. Farah had played the same eight bars forty times that morning, and on the forty-first, with her mother's single word floating in from the kitchen, "again", something inside her had simply given way. She had put the violin down on the bed, so hard that the strings rang, and walked into the kitchen.

"I hate it," she had said. "I hate it, and I hate the way you listen to me, and I'm never playing again." And then, because she was fifteen and could not stop once she had started, she had said worse things. She had said that her mother only cared about the music. She had said that her mother had given up her own violin and now wanted Farah to suffer for it. She had said, finally, that she wished her mother had never taken the stupid thing down from the closet.

Mahin had said nothing at all, which was worse. The violin had gone back into its case, and the case had gone back on top of the closet, and neither of them had mentioned it again for twenty-five years.

Then Leila had been born, and had grown, and at six had found the case at her grandmother's apartment and asked what was inside. And Mahin, who was seventy by then and slightly smaller than she used to be, had taken it down for the second time in her life.

Farah had not objected. If Leila were ever to stop, she had promised herself, she would not say a word. She had told herself that she was letting her daughter make her own choice. That this was different. That Leila had asked. All of which was true, and none of which was the whole truth.

Chapter 3: The Conversation After Dinner

They talked in Leila's room, with the door half closed and the sounds of Marek attempting to bathe Kuba coming faintly down the hall.

Leila was sitting cross-legged on her bed, holding her pillow in front of her like a shield. The violin was on its stand in the corner, beside a music stand covered in photocopied sheets with Ms. Adeyemi's pencil marks all over them. On the wall above the desk was a drawing Leila had done when she was seven: a girl with an enormous violin, almost as tall as she was, and musical notes floating out of it like balloons.

"Is it Ms. Adeyemi?" Farah asked. "Has something happened in lessons?"

"No. Ms. Adeyemi is great."

"Is it the practice? Because if the practice is too much, we could..."

"It's not the practice." Leila hugged the pillow tighter. "I don't mind practicing. Sometimes I like it."

"Then what is it, love?"

Leila looked at the violin for a long time before answering. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"I don't like who I am when I play it," she said.

Farah had been prepared for many answers. She had not been prepared for that one.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean..." Leila struggled. "When I play, I'm always listening for what's wrong. Every note. Is it in tune, is the bow straight, is my wrist right. And then at the end Bunbun says 'good', or she doesn't say anything, and I can tell from her face whether it was good enough." Bunbun was Leila's name for her grandmother; nobody remembered where it had come from. "And I keep thinking, even when it's good, it isn't the kind of good she means. Like there's a good that I'm supposed to reach and I'm never going to reach it."

She stopped, embarrassed. Farah sat very still on the edge of the bed. Outside in the hall, Kuba was singing a song about a shark.

It was so exactly what she herself had felt at fifteen, and had never been able to say, that for a moment she could not speak at all.

"Does that make sense?" Leila asked.

"Yes," said Farah. "It makes a lot of sense."

"Are you angry?"

"No." She reached out and pushed a strand of hair back from her daughter's forehead. "No, I'm not angry. I'm just thinking."

"About what?"

About many things, Farah wanted to say. About a kitchen in another suburb, thirty years ago, and a woman sitting with her tea, listening. About how easily she might have handed her daughter the same gift she had been given, without first checking whether it had ever been a gift.

"About what you said," she answered instead. "Let's both think about it this week. You'll play in the recital, like you promised. And then we'll decide together. Is that fair?"

Leila considered this with the seriousness of a diplomat. "Fair," she said. "But I'm not going to change my mind."

"Then you won't," said Farah. "That's allowed too."

Later, in bed, with the house finally quiet, Marek lay on his back and looked at the ceiling while Farah told him what Leila had said.

"I don't like who I am when I play it," he repeated slowly. "That's quite a sentence for a ten-year-old."

"It's quite a sentence for anyone."

"Did you say it? When you stopped?"

"I didn't say anything useful," said Farah. "I shouted. I was fifteen. I said she cared more about the violin than about me." She was silent for a moment. "I'm not sure I was wrong. I'm not sure I was right, either."

Marek turned his head on the pillow to look at her. In the half-dark, his face was hard to read. "You know what my grandmother used to say? About the dumplings?"

"That they should be thinner than yours?"

"That too. But she said the recipe isn't the important part. The important part is who you're cooking for." He yawned enormously. "I think she was talking about dumplings. But maybe not only."

"Go to sleep, Marek."

"I am asleep," said Marek. "This is all a dream. In the morning you'll find I've made breakfast and cleaned the ceiling." He was snoring within a minute, and he had done neither, and Farah lay awake beside him for a long time, listening to the house.

Chapter 4: A Visit to Mahin

Mahin lived in a small, bright apartment in a building full of retired people, twenty minutes away by tram. She had lived there since Farah's father had died, and she had filled it with plants, photographs and an astonishing number of books in three languages, most of which she had read at least twice. On Thursday afternoons, Farah usually stopped by after her shift. They drank tea and talked about the children, the hospital, the neighbors and the price of saffron, and they did not talk about anything that mattered. This had been the arrangement for many years, and it suited them both.

This Thursday, Farah sat with her cup going cold and said nothing at all for so long that her mother eventually put down her own cup and looked at her.

"Something has happened," Mahin said. It was not a question.

"Leila wants to stop violin."

Mahin was quiet for a moment. Then she nodded slowly, as though Farah had told her that it might rain tomorrow. "When?"

"After the recital."

"I see." Mahin picked up her cup again and looked into it. "And you? What do you want?"

"I don't know," said Farah. "That's why I'm here."

That was not entirely true. What she had come to say, she had been carrying around for twenty-five years, and it had become so heavy and so familiar that she had almost stopped noticing its weight. Now, sitting in her mother's apartment, she noticed it.

"She says she doesn't like who she is when she plays," Farah said. "She says she's always listening for mistakes. That she's always trying to reach a kind of 'good' that she'll never reach." She paused. "Your kind of good, Maman."

The words sat between them on the small table, beside the plate of pistachio cookies. Mahin did not move.

"She said that to you," Mahin said finally. "Or you are saying it to me?"

"Both," said Farah. "I suppose both."

For a long time, neither of them said a word. A tram went past in the street below. Somewhere in the building, someone was playing a radio.

When Mahin spoke again, her voice was different: lower, and less certain than Farah had ever heard it.

"Do you know why I stopped?" she asked.

"No. You never told me."

"No. I never told anyone." Mahin put her cup down carefully, as if it were very fragile. "It was not because of your father, or because of leaving, though that is what everyone assumed. It was because I could no longer hear the music. I could only hear the mistakes. Mine, and everyone else's. In the orchestra, at home, everywhere. My teacher had trained me to listen that way, and his teacher had trained him. Beautiful, it was, the way we played. Beautiful and unhappy. By the end, I would sit in a concert hall and feel nothing but fear."

Farah stared at her.

"When we came here," Mahin went on, "I thought, I will not play again. And then you were eight, and I looked at you, and I thought, she will have what I had, but without the fear. I will give her the good part." She laughed, a short, sad laugh. "And then I sat in the kitchen and listened to you exactly the way my teacher listened to me. I could hear myself doing it. I could not stop. It was like a language I had spoken so long that I could not think in any other."

"Why didn't you ever tell me?"

"Because you were fifteen and furious, and then you were twenty and gone, and then you were thirty and busy, and then you had children of your own." Mahin shrugged, the old, elegant shrug. "And because I was ashamed. Had I known how to listen differently, I would have. I didn't know how. I am not sure I know even now."

It was the longest speech Farah had ever heard her mother make. She found that her eyes were stinging, and that she did not mind.

"Leila thinks you're disappointed in her," she said.

Mahin closed her eyes. "Then I have done it twice," she said. "I suggest you tell her she can stop. Today, if she wants. It is essential that Leila choose for herself, and it is important that she not carry it the way you carried it. The way I carried it."

"She wants to play in the recital first. She promised her teacher."

"Of course she does." Mahin opened her eyes, and for a moment something like a smile crossed her face. "She is your daughter. And mine. We always keep our promises, even the stupid ones."

On Friday, before her night shift, Farah went to see Ms. Adeyemi.

The teacher lived in a house full of instruments, on a quiet street of jacaranda trees whose purple flowers covered the sidewalk like a carpet. She made coffee in a small brass pot and listened without interrupting while Farah explained. When Farah had finished, Ms. Adeyemi did not look surprised.

"I wondered when this would come," she said. "She's been thinking about it since the summer, I think. She plays beautifully, you know. Not perfectly. Beautifully. They aren't the same thing, and the difference is the whole of music."

"If she were to stop, would she regret it?"

Ms. Adeyemi laughed. "Regret is not my business. I teach the violin, not your family. But I'll tell you something I've noticed in thirty years of teaching." She put down her cup. "The students who stop lessons at ten or eleven usually stop playing forever. Unless something else is going on."

"Something else?"

"Has she told you about the tunes?"

Farah shook her head.

"She makes them up," said Ms. Adeyemi. "At the end of every lesson, when she thinks I'm not listening, she plays little melodies of her own. Eight bars. Twelve. Sometimes they're very good." She smiled. "I've never mentioned it, because I think if I did, she'd stop. Some things are better not watched too closely."

Farah walked home under the jacaranda trees, slowly, thinking about her daughter alone in the teacher's front room, playing small tunes for nobody. She had never once, in four years, heard Leila do that at home. It occurred to her, with a sharp, uncomfortable pang, that perhaps Leila had never felt it was safe to.

Chapter 5: Practice

That week, Leila practiced every evening, as she always did, for forty-five minutes. The piece she was preparing for the recital was a short, sweet romance by a composer nobody in the family had heard of before Ms. Adeyemi brought it in. It had a slow, singing middle section that climbed and sank like someone breathing, and it was, Leila said, the only piece she had ever learned that she actually liked.

Farah found herself listening from the kitchen. She tried, at first, not to. She turned on the faucet, or the radio, or asked Kuba long questions about sharks. But the music came through the walls anyway, and after a while she stopped fighting it, and simply sat at the kitchen table and listened.

What she noticed, after the second night, was something she had never noticed before. She was not listening for mistakes. She heard them, of course. A note in the middle section that was slightly sharp. A moment where the bow scraped. But they did not seem to matter very much.

What she heard, much more clearly, was her daughter. She heard the slight hesitation before the high note, as though Leila were taking a breath before stepping off a ledge. She heard the way the slow section always slowed down a little more than it was supposed to, because Leila loved it and did not want it to end.

On the third night, Marek came and sat down opposite her with two cups of tea.

"You're listening differently," he said.

She looked at him in surprise. Marek was, by his own cheerful admission, completely tone-deaf. He sang to the children in a voice that could strip paint, and he had once mistaken a recording of a string quartet for a malfunctioning washing machine.

"How would you know?" she said, smiling.

"Not to the music. I can't tell the music from the fridge. To her." He pushed one of the cups toward her. "Before, you used to sit like you were waiting for something to go wrong. Like at work, when you're watching a monitor. Now you're just sitting."

Farah was silent for a while.

"My mother told me why she stopped," she said at last.

Marek did not ask what she meant. He simply waited, which was one of the many things she loved him for, and after a while she told him. When she had finished, he was quiet for a long time.

"So what are you going to do?" he asked.

"Let her stop, if she still wants to."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

He shrugged, and looked at the ceiling, where a small white patch of flour from Tuesday was still visible. "You've been listening to her play for four years," he said. "You stopped at fifteen. You're forty. I just wonder sometimes whether it's Leila who should be making the big decision this week, or not only Leila."

Farah stared at him. "I'm not going to start playing the violin again at forty, Marek."

"Of course not," said Marek, in the tone of voice of a man who has said what he came to say and knows when to leave. He finished his tea and stood up. "I'm going to read Kuba the shark book for the eleventh time. I'm told there's a twist."

On Friday evening, Mahin telephoned. It was not her usual time, and Farah answered with a small flicker of alarm. But her mother only wanted to speak to Leila.

Farah handed over the phone and went into the kitchen, and tried very hard not to listen. She managed it, mostly. She heard only her daughter's side of the conversation, and only fragments of it. "Yes. I know. Really? You never told me that. Okay. Okay, Bunbun. I will."

When Leila came into the kitchen to give back the phone, her face was thoughtful.

"What did Bunbun say?" Farah asked, as casually as she could.

"She said that when she was in the orchestra, before concerts, she used to be sick with nerves," said Leila. "Actually sick. Every time. She said that's normal and doesn't mean anything." She paused. "And she said that tomorrow, she's not going to listen for mistakes. She said she's going to listen like a person who's never heard a violin before."

"Can she do that?"

"I don't know," said Leila. "She said she was going to practice." And with that, she went off to bed, leaving her mother standing in the kitchen with the phone in her hand and an expression that Marek, coming in a moment later, described as "like someone who's just seen a ghost, but a nice one."

Chapter 6: The Recital

The recital was held on a Saturday afternoon in the hall of a community center, a large room with a wooden floor, plastic chairs and a piano that had seen better days. Ms. Adeyemi, a tall, unhurried woman with silver earrings and a voice that could calm a room of thirty nervous children, stood at the front and welcomed everyone.

Mahin came. She sat in the second row, beside Farah, in a dark green dress and her best earrings, with her handbag on her knees. She had not spoken about their conversation since Thursday, and Farah had not asked her to. But when Mahin arrived, she had done something she had not done in many years: she had taken her daughter's hand and held it, briefly, before sitting down.

The children played in order of age, from the youngest to the oldest. There were tiny violinists of six who played three-note tunes with fierce concentration and bowed so deeply that they nearly fell over. There was a boy of eight who played a lively folk dance far too fast and finished, red-faced and triumphant, several seconds before the piano. There was a girl of nine who forgot the middle of her piece, stood frozen for a long, terrible moment and then, at a whispered word from Ms. Adeyemi, began again from the start and played it perfectly. The applause for her was the loudest of the afternoon.

Then it was Leila's turn.

Before her turn, Leila had come back to the second row to sit with them for a minute, as the older children were allowed to do. She sat between her mother and her grandmother, very straight, with her violin case on her knees. Her fingers were drumming silently on the lid.

"I feel sick," she whispered.

"That's normal," Mahin whispered back. "It doesn't mean anything."

"What if I forget the middle, like that girl?"

"Then you begin again, like that girl," said Mahin. "And everybody claps more." She considered this for a moment. "Actually, that would not be such a bad plan."

Leila giggled, nervously, and something in her shoulders came down an inch. Then Ms. Adeyemi called her name, and she stood up.

She walked to the front of the room with her violin under her arm, and Farah saw that she was pale. Ms. Adeyemi sat down at the piano. Leila lifted the violin to her shoulder, put the bow on the string and looked out at the audience. For a moment, her eyes found her grandmother's in the second row.

Farah felt her mother's hand tighten on her own.

And then Mahin did something extraordinary. She smiled, a wide, open, completely unguarded smile, the kind of smile Farah had seen on her face perhaps five times in her life, and she gave her granddaughter a small nod, as if to say: "Go on. Enjoy it. Nothing else matters."

Leila began to play.

Perfect it was not. The first high note was slightly sharp. The bow scraped once, at the start of the second phrase, loudly enough for a small child in the front row to giggle. But then she came to the slow middle section, and something happened that Farah would never forget.

Leila closed her eyes. She slowed down, more than she was supposed to, more than Ms. Adeyemi had marked in pencil, and she let each note go on for a moment longer than necessary, as though she were reluctant to let it leave. Ms. Adeyemi, at the piano, heard it at once and slowed down with her, following her, letting Leila set the pace.

The room went very quiet. Even the giggling child in the front row was still.

When it ended, there was a pause of perhaps two seconds before anyone clapped. Farah had been to many concerts. She knew what that pause meant.

Beside her, Mahin was weeping, silently, with her handbag still on her knees and her face completely calm. Farah had never seen her mother cry. Nor, she realized, had she ever seen her look so happy.

Chapter 7: After

They went for ice cream afterward, all five of them, at a small shop near the tram stop that had a line out of the door. Kuba had three flavors and got most of them on his shirt. Marek had one flavor and got most of it on his shirt anyway. Mahin had a small cup of pistachio and ate it slowly with a tiny plastic spoon, watching her granddaughter.

Leila had not said anything about violin since the recital. She had accepted everyone's congratulations politely and packed up her instrument with great care, and she was now eating a large chocolate cone with the concentration of someone who has earned it.

Finally, it was Mahin who spoke.

"Leila jan," she said. "I would like to tell you something, and I would like you to listen, and then I would like you to forget I said it, if you want to."

Leila looked up.

"Today, when you played the slow part, you played it wrong," said Mahin. "Much too slowly. Far more slowly than it is written."

Farah's heart sank. Marek put down his ice cream. Leila's face began to close, the way it always closed when she heard the word "wrong".

"And it was the most beautiful music I have heard in forty years," said Mahin. "It was more beautiful than anything I ever played in a concert hall. Because you were not afraid. Do you understand? I have been listening to music the wrong way my whole life. Today you taught me how to listen to it the right way." She put down her plastic spoon. "Whatever you decide, I am grateful for that. I wanted you to know."

There was a long silence at the table. Leila looked at her grandmother, then at her mother, then down at her ice cream, which was starting to melt over her fingers.

"I still want to stop lessons," she said, in a small voice. "I'm sorry, Bunbun."

"Don't be sorry. It is your music, not mine. It is essential that you choose it yourself."

"But I don't want to stop playing," Leila went on, a little louder. "I just want to stop... the lessons. The exams. The every-day-forty-five-minutes. I want to play the slow parts, slowly. Is that allowed?"

Mahin looked at Farah, and Farah looked back at her mother, and it seemed to Farah that something very old, which had been tied tight for thirty years, came quietly undone between them.

"That's allowed," said Farah.

"Can I still keep the violin, though? At home?"

"It's your violin," said Mahin. "It has been yours since you were six. I only borrowed it before you."

Leila considered this. Then she turned to her mother with the expression of a diplomat who has one final clause to add to a treaty.

"Then can I ask something else?"

"Of course."

"You used to play too. Bunbun told me. When you were my age." Leila's eyes were very steady. "Would you play with me sometimes? Not lessons. Just playing. You could have the second part. Ms. Adeyemi has easy duets."

Farah opened her mouth, and closed it again. Across the table, Marek was studying the menu above the counter with tremendous concentration, as though it contained the secrets of the universe.

"I haven't played in twenty-five years," Farah said. "If I were to pick up a violin now, I'd be terrible."

"I know," said Leila cheerfully. "That's the point. Then we can both be terrible together, and nobody has to listen for mistakes."

Kuba, who had been listening to all of this while slowly eating the last of his third flavor, looked up.

"Can I stop something now?" he asked.

"And if you were to stop something, Kuba, what would it be?" said Mahin, with great seriousness.

Kuba thought hard. "Baths," he said.

It was Marek who laughed first, a huge, helpless laugh that made the people at the next table turn around. Then Leila laughed, and then Farah, and finally, to everyone's astonishment, Mahin, who laughed until she had to wipe her eyes with a paper napkin and her earrings shook.

The following Sunday morning, a cheap second violin, rented for a month from a music shop in the city, sat on the kitchen table beside the dumpling flour, which Marek had still not quite managed to clean off the ceiling. Farah lifted it to her shoulder. It felt strange, and heavy, and astonishingly familiar, like a word in a language you thought you had forgotten.

She had told her mother about the rented violin on the phone the night before, a little defensively, as though confessing to something. There had been a pause on the line.

"Do you want me to come and listen?" Mahin had asked.

"No," Farah had said, and then, more gently: "Not yet, Maman. Maybe in a few months. When we're ready."

"Good," said her mother, surprising her completely. "Don't let me in until you are terrible together and happy about it. Then I will come and I will bring cookies, and I will not say one single word." She paused. "Perhaps one. 'Breathe.' But only that one."

From the next room came the sound of her daughter tuning up.

"Ready?" Leila called.

Farah put the bow on the string. Her hands were shaking a little. In her head, very faintly, she could hear her mother's voice from thirty years ago: "Slower. Again. Breathe." But the voice did not frighten her this time. It sounded, if anything, like encouragement.

"Ready," she said. And they began, not well, but together.
