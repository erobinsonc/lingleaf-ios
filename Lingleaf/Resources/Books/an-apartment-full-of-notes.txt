Chapter 1: The Key That Sticks

The first note was taped to the inside of the front door, at exactly the height of Meera's eyes. It said, in small, careful capital letters: WELCOME. THE KEY STICKS. LIFT IT A LITTLE, THEN TURN. DON'T FIGHT IT.

She lifted it a little, then turned, and the door gave way with a soft click. Meera Kulkarni stood in the doorway of a stranger's apartment with two suitcases, a backpack and the slightly unreal feeling of someone who has been awake for twenty-six hours. Behind her, the stairwell smelled of floor polish and somebody's dinner. In front of her, the apartment was full of light.

It was a third-floor walk-up in an old brick building in Montreal. It had tall windows, wooden floors that creaked in a friendly way and a kitchen barely big enough for one person to turn around in.

She had found it online, in a listing that said only: "Summer sublet. Quiet. Plants included. Musician away on tour." The rent was lower than anything else in the neighborhood. Meera had just accepted a twelve-week internship at a transit-planning office downtown, and she had not been in a position to ask many questions.

What she noticed after the light were the notes. They were everywhere. There was one on the fridge about the recycling schedule, and one on the bathroom mirror explaining that the hot water took exactly forty seconds to arrive. On the bookshelf, a note said, PLEASE BORROW ANYTHING. PLEASE PUT IT BACK SOMEWHERE NEAR WHERE IT WAS. By the window, propped against an old radio, a longer note had been folded into a small tent.

She opened it. It was addressed to her by name, which was strange. It was signed by Kofi Mensah, the man whose life she would be living in for the summer.

"Dear Meera," it began. "Thank you for looking after the place. Most of the plants are tough and will forgive you anything. The exception is the fern by the window. Her name is Abena. She needs water every Sunday, not too much, and she likes the radio on in the mornings. I know how that sounds. Please humor me. Kofi."

Meera looked at the fern. It sat in a heavy blue pot on a wooden stool, its long green fronds spilling over the edges like water from a fountain. It was, she had to admit, a beautiful plant. It was also, as far as she could tell, the only living thing in the apartment that had been given a name.

"Hello, Abena," she said, feeling ridiculous. Then she sat down on the floor, leaned against the sofa and fell asleep with her shoes still on.

Chapter 2: Sundays

For the first two weeks, she did everything the notes told her to do. On Sunday mornings she filled the small copper watering can, gave Abena exactly half of it and turned on the radio. It was permanently tuned to a station that played jazz in the mornings and talked about the weather in two languages. She lifted the key a little before she turned it. She waited forty seconds for the hot water.

It was a kind of company, she realized later. Meera had grown up in Nagpur in an apartment where there were always at least five people talking at once. There were her parents, her grandmother, her younger brother and usually a cousin or two who had dropped by and stayed for dinner. She had moved to Toronto for her master's degree and shared a house with four other students. She had never, in twenty-four years, lived entirely alone. The notes filled up the silence in a way that she would not have been able to explain to anyone.

Work was a different matter. The office was on the ninth floor of a glass building near the river. It was full of clever, friendly people who all seemed to know exactly what they were doing. Meera had been hired to help build a model that predicted how many people would use a new bus line.

On her first day, her manager, a tall, softly spoken woman named Ingrid Solberg, had shown her a spreadsheet with four hundred thousand rows. Then she had said, "Don't worry, nobody understands this yet. That's why you're here."

It was meant to be reassuring. It was not. By the end of the first week, Meera had worked out three things. The data was messy, the deadline was in August, and she was the only person on the team who had never built a model like this outside a classroom.

The only person who seemed to notice was Tomás Pinto, a junior engineer from Porto who sat at the next desk and ate the same cheese sandwich every day. "You know you can go home," he said one evening, as he put on his jacket. "The buses will still be late tomorrow, whether you fix the model tonight or not."

"I just want to get on top of it," said Meera.

"Nobody gets on top of it," said Tomás. "You just get used to being underneath it." He meant it as a joke, and she laughed politely, but she stayed until nine anyway.

She began staying late. Then she began staying very late. She ate dinner from containers at her desk and walked home along empty streets after ten.

Her mother called every evening at what was breakfast time in Nagpur. At first Meera answered every time. Then she began to let some of the calls go, telling herself that she would call back when she had something good to report. "Busy day," she would type. "Talk tomorrow." Her mother would reply with a single heart, which was somehow worse than a complaint.

On the third Sunday, Meera woke up late, with her laptop open on the bed beside her. She remembered the fern while she was brushing her teeth, ran into the living room and watered it with the toothbrush still in her mouth. She forgot the radio. She told herself it didn't matter.

On the fourth Sunday, she went into the office.

Chapter 3: Brown at the Edges

It was the smell she noticed first, a dry, dusty smell, like paper that has been in the sun too long. Then she saw it. Half of Abena's fronds had turned a pale, crisp brown at the edges. A few had collapsed completely and hung down the side of the blue pot like broken umbrellas. When Meera touched one, it crumbled between her fingers.

She stood in the middle of the room with her bag still on her shoulder and she felt her face go hot. It had been ten days. Maybe eleven. A heatwave had come in the week before, and she had kept the windows closed and the blinds open because she was never home in the daytime anyway. If she had thought about it for even a minute, she would have moved the plant out of the sun. If she had read the note again, she would have remembered that "not too much" did not mean "not at all."

"Okay," she said aloud. "Okay. This is fine."

It was not fine. She gave the fern a full glass of water, then worried that it was too much, then searched online and she read eleven articles that contradicted one another completely. One said brown fronds could never recover. One said that ferns were "remarkably resilient" and showed a photograph of a plant that looked nothing like Abena. A third suggested that she talk to it. She closed her laptop.

What frightened her, she understood later that night, was not really the plant. It was the feeling that she had let something slip without noticing, and that she was the kind of person who let things slip. In the office, she was behind on the model. At home, she was behind on her mother. And now, in a stranger's apartment, she had managed to neglect the one thing she had been specifically asked to care for.

At eleven that night her laptop gave a soft chime. It was an email from Kofi Mensah. The subject line said: "Small change of plans."

"Hi Meera," he wrote. "I hope the apartment is treating you well. The tour has been cut short. Our pianist broke his wrist, nothing serious but no more concerts this summer. I'll be back in Montreal in about three weeks. I've arranged to stay with my sister until your sublet ends, so please don't worry. I might just stop by to pick up some music I left behind. How is Abena?"

Meera read the last line four times. Then she shut the laptop, turned off the light and she lay awake until the birds started.

The next morning at work, she stared at her screen for an hour without typing anything. At lunch, Tomás put half of his cheese sandwich on her desk without a word. It was such a small thing that she almost cried.

"Is it the model?" he asked.

"It's a fern," she said.

He thought about this for a moment. "My grandmother had a plant doctor," he said. "In Porto, everyone does. You probably have one here too. People who know plants always know other people who know plants." He went back to his desk, and she sat looking at the sandwich, and then she searched for plant shops near the apartment. There was one ten minutes away. It closed at seven.

Chapter 4: The Plant Doctor

The plant shop was on a side street ten minutes from the apartment, between a bakery and a shop that repaired bicycles. Meera had walked past it every day without going in. On Monday evening, she left work at six for the first time in a month and carried Abena there in her arms, pot and all, like a sick child.

The shop was small, warm and green, and so full of plants that it seemed to be breathing. Behind the counter sat a woman of about seventy with short white hair and reading glasses on a chain around her neck. A small sign by the register said: MARGUERITE. PLANT CARE AND OPINIONS.

"Oh dear," said Marguerite, when she saw the fern. "Oh dear, oh dear. Put her down here. What happened?"

Meera told her everything, the heatwave and the closed windows and the Sundays she had missed. She had not meant to tell her about the job, or the four hundred thousand rows, or her mother's single hearts, but it all came out at once. When she finished, she was slightly out of breath and very embarrassed.

Marguerite had listened without interrupting. Now she lifted one of the brown fronds, turned it over and looked carefully at the base of the plant, where the stems disappeared into the soil.

"Look here," she said. She pointed to the very center of the pot. Meera leaned closer. Down among the dead stems, almost hidden, were three or four tiny green shoots, each one curled tight like the top of a violin.

"Those are fiddleheads," said Marguerite. "New fronds. As long as she's making those, she isn't dead. She's just very, very annoyed with you."

Meera laughed, and was surprised to find that her eyes were wet.

"Here's what we do," Marguerite went on. "We cut her back. All the brown goes. She'll look terrible for a few weeks, and you'll want to give up on her. Don't. Keep her out of direct sun, water her a little, often, and leave her alone. Ferns don't like being fussed over. They like being noticed. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

Marguerite looked at her over the top of her glasses. "Fussing is when you panic and do a lot of things. Noticing is when you come home every day and look." She picked up a pair of scissors. "Do you want to do it, or should I?"

"Could you?" said Meera. "I think if I did it, I would cut off the wrong ones."

It took twenty minutes. When it was finished, Abena was a small, untidy bundle of green stems no taller than Meera's hand. It was impossible to imagine that it had ever spilled over the edges of anything.

"She looks so small," Meera said.

"She is small, for now," said Marguerite. "If you had brought her in a week later, we would be having a very different conversation. But you didn't, so we aren't. That's what recovery looks like. Nobody takes photographs of it." She wrapped the pot in brown paper to protect it on the walk home. "One more thing. Turn the radio on in the mornings."

Meera stared at her. "How did you know about the radio?"

"I didn't," said Marguerite. "But you said she belonged to a musician. Musicians always leave the radio on for their plants. It isn't for the plants, of course. It's for the musicians."

She carried the fern home slowly, through streets that were still warm from the day. People were sitting outside cafés and on the steps of their buildings, talking and laughing in French and English and several other languages she didn't recognize. She realized that she had lived in this neighborhood for six weeks and had never once walked through it in daylight without hurrying. It was, she thought, a little like the fern. She had been so busy keeping up that she had forgotten to look.

Chapter 5: Mr. Haddad's Story

On the stairs she met the neighbor from the second floor, a short, round man in his seventies who was carrying a bag of oranges and breathing heavily. She had seen him before but had never done more than nod. He stopped when he saw the paper-wrapped pot in her arms.

"Is that Kofi's fern?" he asked. "Abena?"

"You know her?"

"Everybody in this building knows Abena." He put his oranges down on the step and held out his hand. "Samir Haddad. Second floor. I keep a key for Kofi, in case of emergencies. Is this an emergency?"

"It was," said Meera. "I think it's turning into a recovery."

Mr. Haddad insisted that she come in for tea. His apartment was the same shape as Kofi's but entirely different in feeling: thick rugs, heavy furniture, photographs on every wall and a bowl of pistachios on every table. He made the tea strong and sweet and put the oranges in a bowl as if they were decorations.

"So you nearly killed her," he said cheerfully, sitting down opposite her. "Don't look like that. He nearly killed her too, the first winter. Twice."

"He did?"

"Of course. He was twenty-three, he had just come from Ghana, he had never seen snow and he put her right next to the radiator." Mr. Haddad shook his head. "It was my wife who saved her. She brought her down here for a month. Kofi came every evening to visit. Like visiting someone in the hospital."

Meera held her cup with both hands. "Why does it matter so much to him? It's a beautiful plant, but..."

Mr. Haddad was quiet for a moment. "Did he tell you why she's called Abena?"

"No."

"Abena was his grandmother. In Kumasi. She raised him, more or less, while his parents were working in another city. She had a garden, a big one, full of ferns and other things I don't know the names of. When he got the scholarship to study music here, she cut a small piece from her favorite fern. She wrapped it in a wet cloth and put it in his bag. He told me he was terrified the whole flight that they would find it and take it away from him." He smiled. "They didn't find it. She died two years later. He couldn't go back for the funeral, because of the money, and because of the visa, and because of everything. So." He gestured upward, toward the third floor. "So the plant is not exactly a plant."

Meera looked down at the small bundle in brown paper beside her chair. She thought about the toothbrush in her mouth on the third Sunday, and the office on the fourth.

"If I had known that," she said slowly, "I would have been much more careful."

"Would you?" Mr. Haddad asked. His tone was gentle, but he was watching her closely. "Or would you just have been more frightened?"

She opened her mouth to answer, and she found that she didn't know.

"It isn't knowing the story that keeps a plant alive," he said. "It's the Sundays. Anyway, you've brought her back. That's the part he will care about." He pushed the bowl of pistachios toward her. "Eat something. You look like you've been living on coffee."

She had been. She ate a handful of pistachios and then, because he insisted, two oranges. When she went upstairs it was nearly ten o'clock, and she realized she had not thought about the transit model once all evening.

That night, for the first time in nearly two weeks, she called her mother instead of waiting to be called.

"Meera!" Her mother's voice was full of surprise, and then, immediately, of worry. "Is everything okay? Did something happen?"

"Nothing happened," said Meera. Then, because that wasn't true: "Actually, I nearly killed a fern."

There was a pause on the line. Then her mother began to laugh, a long, helpless laugh that went on so long that her grandmother came to the phone to find out what was funny. Meera ended up telling the whole story twice, once in English and once in Marathi, and by the end of it she was laughing too.

Chapter 6: Things That Grow Back

She did not tell Kofi immediately. She wrote three emails and deleted all of them. The first one was too short. The second one was so long and so apologetic that it read like a confession to a crime. The third one simply said, "Abena is fine," which was not exactly a lie, and she deleted it for that reason.

In the end, what she sent was a photograph. It showed the small, cut-back plant in its blue pot, with the radio behind it and the morning light on the stems. Underneath it she wrote: "I made a mistake during the heatwave, and she lost most of her fronds. A woman at the plant shop helped me cut her back. There are four new fiddleheads. I'm sorry. I'm looking after her properly now. Mr. Haddad says to tell you that you nearly killed her twice yourself."

His reply came within the hour.

"Samir is a terrible gossip," Kofi wrote. "He is also correct. Thank you for telling me, and thank you for taking her to Marguerite. I would have done exactly the same thing, only I would have done it a week later, after crying about it. Four fiddleheads is very good news. Please keep the radio on."

Something in Meera's chest, which she had not known was tight, loosened all at once.

Things at work did not suddenly become easy. But it was in the following weeks that she began to do something she had never done before: she asked for help before she was drowning. One morning she went to Ingrid's desk. "I don't understand why the model keeps overestimating the evening numbers, and I've been trying to work it out alone for nine days." Ingrid pulled up a chair, and in forty minutes they found the problem. It turned out to be a single column that counted some passengers twice.

"Why didn't you ask me last week?" Ingrid said, not unkindly.

"Because I thought I was supposed to know," Meera said.

"Nobody is supposed to know," said Ingrid. "That's what teams are for."

She left the office at six most evenings after that. At first it felt like cheating, and she kept checking her email on the bus. Then, slowly, it began to feel normal. The model, to her surprise, did not get worse because she slept more. If anything, it got better.

"What changed?" Tomás asked one afternoon, when she showed the team a chart that finally made sense.

"I stopped pretending I already knew everything," she said. It was true, and it was also the first time she had said it aloud, and she found that it was not as frightening as she had expected.

On Sundays she took long walks up the mountain in the middle of the city. On Thursdays she had tea with Mr. Haddad, who taught her to play backgammon and beat her every single time. She went back to the plant shop and bought a small, nameless succulent for her own desk at work. Marguerite approved.

The Thursday evenings with Mr. Haddad became the part of the week she looked forward to most. He played backgammon fast and talked the whole time. He talked about his wife, who had died four years earlier, and about his son in Beirut and his daughter in Calgary. Most of all he talked about the building, which had been his home since before Kofi was born.

"You know what my wife used to say?" he asked one evening, moving his pieces around the board so quickly that she could barely follow. "She said that people are like ferns. They don't need much. But they need it regularly."

"That sounds like something Marguerite would say."

"Of course. They were friends for thirty years. Where do you think Marguerite got it from?" He threw the dice and won again. "If my wife had met you, she would have adopted you on the first day. She would have made you eat three meals a day and phone your mother every evening."

"I do phone my mother now," said Meera.

"I know," said Mr. Haddad. "The walls in this building are very thin."

And every morning, she looked at Abena. She didn't fuss. She noticed. The fiddleheads uncurled, slowly, one after another, into small, bright fronds the color of spring grass. By the middle of August there were eleven of them. By the end of August the plant had begun, very slightly, to spill over the edge of the pot again.

Kofi came by on the last Saturday of the month to pick up his music. He was tall and thin, with a careful, quiet voice and a cast on one thumb. He said it had nothing to do with the pianist's wrist and was a long, stupid story involving a suitcase. He stood in the doorway for a while without coming in, the way people do when they are visiting their own home as a guest.

Then he saw the fern.

He crossed the room and crouched down beside her and looked for a long time without saying anything. Meera had prepared several speeches, and she realized that she did not need any of them.

"She's smaller," he said at last.

"She's growing back," said Meera.

"She is." He touched one of the new fronds very lightly with his finger, the way you might touch the shoulder of someone asleep. "You know, if you hadn't told me, I would never have known. She looks happy."

"I would have known," said Meera.

He looked up at her and smiled. It was the smile, more than anything he said, that made her understand why someone might carry a piece of a garden across an ocean.

On her last night in the apartment, she packed her two suitcases and her backpack and cleaned the kitchen until it shone. It was a Sunday. She filled the small copper watering can, gave Abena exactly half of it and turned on the radio. Then she sat down on the floor with her back against the sofa, where she had fallen asleep on the first night, and she called her mother.

"So you are coming home for your cousin's wedding?" her mother asked. "Not just a message, saying you're busy?"

"I'm coming home," said Meera. "And Amma, I'm going to bring Ajji a plant."

"Your grandmother kills every plant she touches," said her mother.

"I know," said Meera. "So I'll stay a week and show her how to look after it. Not fuss. Just notice."

There was a pause on the line. Behind her mother's voice, Meera could hear the familiar noise of the apartment in Nagpur: the pressure cooker, her brother arguing with someone, her grandmother's television.

"Meera," her mother said, more quietly. "This summer, when you didn't call. I thought you were angry with us."

Meera looked at the fern. She thought about the toothbrush in her mouth on the third Sunday, and the single hearts on her phone, and how near she had come to letting everything slip. If she hadn't nearly killed a plant, she thought, she might still be sitting at her desk at ten o'clock every night, pretending she was fine.

"I wasn't angry," she said. "I was drowning. I just didn't know I was allowed to say so."

"It's allowed," said her mother. "It's always allowed. That's what we're for."

Meera closed her eyes. On the radio, the jazz station was talking about the weather in two languages. Beside her, in the blue pot, Abena's newest frond was slowly uncurling toward the light.

"Amma," she said. "Tell me about your day. Everything. I have time."

And for the next hour, in a stranger's apartment that no longer felt like a stranger's, she listened.
