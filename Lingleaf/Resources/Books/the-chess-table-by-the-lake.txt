Chapter 1: Six O'Clock

Mr. Bao has lived in the same narrow house in Hanoi for forty-one years. The house is near a small lake, which is green and calm in the early morning. Every day at six o'clock, Mr. Bao walks around the lake three times. Then he sits down at a stone table under a big old tree.

There is a chessboard painted on the table. The paint is old now, and some of the white squares look gray. Mr. Bao has played chess at this table since he retired from his job as a math teacher. That was fourteen years ago.

For all of those years, he has played with the same man. His name is Mr. Long. Mr. Long used to drive a bus. He still talks about the city as if he can see every street from his seat.

Mr. Long plays fast and he laughs loudly. Mr. Bao plays slowly and almost never laughs. They are a good team.

Around them, the morning is always busy. Women in bright clothes dance to music from a small speaker. A young man sells sticky rice from a basket on his bicycle. Old friends do exercises by the water, raising their arms to the sky. Mr. Bao has watched all of this for many years. It feels like part of his own body.

Chapter 2: News

One Monday in March, Mr. Long arrived late. This was the first time in fourteen years. He sat down, but he didn't open the box of chess pieces.

"My daughter has found a new job in Da Nang," he said. "She has asked me to move there with her family. Her children are small, and she needs help. I have said yes."

Mr. Bao looked at the board for a long time. "When?" he asked.

"In two weeks."

"Da Nang is beautiful," said Mr. Bao. "You will like the sea."

"I know," said Mr. Long. "But there is no Mr. Bao in Da Nang."

They played one last time on the day before Mr. Long moved away. Mr. Bao won, which was unusual. He was not sure that Mr. Long hadn't let him win. At the end, they shook hands like they always did. Then Mr. Long walked away, and Mr. Bao sat alone at the stone table.

Chapter 3: An Empty Chair

The next morning, Mr. Bao went to the lake at six o'clock as usual. He walked around it three times. He sat down at the table. He opened the box and put all the pieces in their places. Then he waited.

Nobody came.

He played against himself for a while, but it was not interesting. He always knew what the other side was planning. He and Mr. Long used to play until the dancers went home. Today, after an hour, he put the pieces back in the box and went home.

His granddaughter Thu lives with him. She is twenty-three and works for a company that designs websites. She works from home, so she is always on her laptop at the kitchen table.

"Grandpa, you're back early," she said.

"There is nothing to do at the lake now," he said.

Thu closed her laptop. She knew how much the morning games meant to him. "Why don't you find a new partner?" she asked. "Lots of people play chess."

"Lots of people play badly," said Mr. Bao.

Thu laughed. "You used to say that about your students too. And then they all became good."

Chapter 4: The Boy with the Bicycle

For a week, Mr. Bao went to the lake and played alone. On the eighth day, something changed.

A teenage boy who delivered food on a bicycle stopped near the table. He was wearing a helmet and a big yellow bag on his back. His phone was in a holder on the handlebars. He was waiting for his next order, and he was watching Mr. Bao's game.

After a few minutes, he spoke. "Sorry, sir. Why didn't you move the horse there?"

"It is called a knight," said Mr. Bao. "And if I move it there, I lose my queen in three moves."

The boy looked at the board. He thought about it. Then his eyes opened wide. "Oh," he said. "Yes. I see it."

Mr. Bao looked at him with new interest. Most people couldn't see three moves ahead. "Do you play?" he asked.

"Only on my phone," said the boy. "I have never played on a real board." His phone made a sound. "Sorry, I have an order." And he rode away.

The next day, the boy came back. And the day after that. His name was Khoa. He was seventeen, and he was saving money for university. He wanted to study engineering.

Every morning, while he was waiting for orders, he sat with Mr. Bao for a few minutes. Mr. Bao taught him one small thing each time. He showed him how the knight moves and how to protect the king. Above all, he taught him to think before he touched a piece.

"You are the first student I have had in fourteen years," Mr. Bao told him one day.

"Is that good or bad?" asked Khoa.

"I haven't decided yet," said Mr. Bao. But he was smiling.

Chapter 5: A Problem with Phones

One evening, Thu found her grandfather in the kitchen. He was holding her old tablet, which she had given him the year before. He never used it.

"Thu," he said. "Can this machine play chess?"

"Of course. There are many apps for chess. Why?"

"Khoa says that people play against each other on these apps," he said. "People who live in different cities. Is it true?"

Thu understood immediately. "Grandpa, do you want to play with Mr. Long?"

Mr. Bao didn't answer. He just held the tablet out to her.

That night, Thu installed an app and made an account for him. She called Mr. Long's daughter in Da Nang, and they did the same thing for Mr. Long. It took two hours, because neither man liked passwords.

"Why do I need a password to play chess?" asked Mr. Bao.

"So nobody else can play your games," said Thu.

"Nobody else wants to play my games," he said. "They are too slow."

Chapter 6: Two Boards

The next morning, Mr. Bao went to the lake at six o'clock. He walked around it three times. He sat down at the stone table. He set up the real chessboard, and next to it he put the tablet.

On the screen was a message: "Mr. Long wants to play. Accept?"

Mr. Bao touched the button carefully with one finger. A small video window opened at the top of the screen. There was Mr. Long, sitting on a balcony with the sea behind him. He was wearing a hat that looked very new.

"Bao!" he shouted, so loudly that the dancing women turned around. "Can you hear me? The sea is very blue! I hate it!"

Mr. Bao laughed. It was a real laugh, the kind that he didn't use very often. "Play," he said. "You are wasting my time."

They played the whole game. Mr. Long moved his pieces on the screen. Mr. Bao copied each move onto the real board, because he liked to touch the pieces. Khoa arrived on his bicycle in the middle of the game and stood behind him, watching.

"Who is that?" asked Mr. Long.

"My student," said Mr. Bao. "He is learning. One day he will beat you."

"Nobody beats me," said Mr. Long.

"I have beaten you many times," said Mr. Bao.

Chapter 7: Every Morning

It is summer now in Hanoi. The mornings are hot, even at six o'clock. Mr. Bao still walks around the lake three times. He still sits at the stone table under the old tree.

On Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, he plays with Mr. Long on the tablet. Mr. Long has become a little slower since he moved to the sea. He says it is the sun. Mr. Bao says it is age. They argue about it happily for ten minutes every time.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays, he plays with Khoa on the real board. Khoa has improved a lot. Last week, he almost won. Mr. Bao had to think very hard for twenty minutes. Khoa's customers waited a little longer for their noodles.

On Saturdays, Thu comes with him. She doesn't play chess. She drinks iced coffee and works on her laptop, and sometimes she looks up and watches her grandfather. He used to look tired in the mornings. He doesn't anymore.

And on Sundays, Mr. Bao plays against himself. It is still not very interesting. But now he doesn't mind, because he knows that tomorrow somebody will be waiting for him.
