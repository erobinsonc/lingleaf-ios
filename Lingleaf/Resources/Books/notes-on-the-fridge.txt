Chapter 1: Room Number Four

Valeria arrived in Rotterdam on a gray afternoon at the end of August, carrying two suitcases and a backpack that was far too heavy. She had never lived outside Guadalajara before. She had never lived with strangers, either. And she had certainly never lived in a city where the wind seemed to come from every direction at once.

The apartment was on the third floor of a narrow brick building near the river. It had four bedrooms, one bathroom and a small living room. The kitchen had clearly been painted yellow by someone who loved the color more than anyone else did. Valeria's room was number four. It was small, but it had a big window that looked out over the water, and she decided immediately that she liked it.

The landlord, a busy man called Mr. Visser, gave her the keys in the doorway. He told her that the rent had to be paid on the first day of every month and that the trash was collected on Tuesdays. Then he said that if she had any problems, she should send him an email. He was gone before she could ask him a single question.

That evening, she went into the kitchen to make some tea. On the fridge, held in place by a magnet shaped like a tulip, there was a piece of paper. Someone had written on it in neat black letters: "PLEASE wash your dishes the same day. Thank you."

Valeria looked around the empty kitchen. There were no dirty dishes anywhere. The note, she realized, was not for her. It was for somebody who had lived there before her. Or it was a warning.

Chapter 2: Three Closed Doors

For the first two weeks, she barely saw her roommates. She knew their names because they were written on a list next to the door, along with a cleaning schedule that nobody seemed to follow. There was Mei, who was studying architecture and was from Taichung. There was Pieter, who was Dutch and was writing a thesis on something to do with water management. And there was Kwame, from Accra, who was doing a master's degree in public health and who apparently never slept.

Valeria heard them more than she saw them. She heard Mei's music late at night, soft piano that came through the thin wall. She heard Pieter's bicycle being carried up the stairs every morning at seven. She heard Kwame on video calls with his family, laughing in a language she didn't understand.

But if she met one of them in the kitchen, the conversation never lasted more than a minute. "Hi." "Hi." "How's it going?" "Good, you?" "Good." And then everybody went back behind their closed doors.

Meanwhile, the notes on the fridge multiplied. A second one appeared in her third week: "Whoever used the last of the milk, please buy more." A third one followed a few days later: "The bathroom has not been cleaned for ten days. The schedule is there for a reason."

Valeria had not used the milk. She had cleaned the bathroom twice, because she had been told by her mother that a clean bathroom was the foundation of a peaceful home. She thought about writing her own note in reply, but she didn't know who she would be answering. All the notes had been written in the same neat black letters, and none of them were signed.

If she had been at home, she would have simply asked. At home, everyone talked. Here, it seemed, people wrote notes and hid.

Chapter 3: The Cold

In October, the weather changed. The wind from the river became sharp and wet, and the days grew shorter. By the middle of November, it was dark at five o'clock. Valeria bought a thick coat, a hat and the warmest socks she could find.

Then, on a Sunday night at the end of November, the heat stopped working.

She noticed it at first only as a faint chill in her room. By the morning, she could see her breath in the air. She put on her coat and went to the kitchen. There she found Kwame standing in front of the radiator with his hand on it, as if he was checking a sick child.

"It's dead," he said. "Completely dead. I think the boiler has broken."

"Has anyone told Mr. Visser?" Valeria asked.

"I sent him an email last night," said Kwame. "No answer yet."

Later that day, Mr. Visser finally replied. He wrote that he was very sorry and that a technician would be sent as soon as possible. He said that the company he used was very busy at this time of year. The problem, he promised, would be solved by Friday at the latest.

Friday came and went. Nobody was sent. Kwame emailed again and was told that the part they needed had been ordered from Germany. Pieter emailed and was told the same thing, in exactly the same words. Mei called Mr. Visser three times, but her calls were never answered.

The apartment grew colder every day. Valeria started wearing her coat indoors and her hat in bed. She did her coursework at the university library, which was warm and stayed open until midnight. She only came home to sleep.

One night, walking home along the river, she called her older brother, Andrés. She told him that the heat had been broken for almost two weeks. He asked her why she hadn't simply knocked on her roommates' doors to make a plan together. Valeria said that it wasn't that kind of apartment. People here didn't knock. They wrote notes.

"Then write a better note," Andrés said. "Or cook something. Nobody can stay behind a closed door when there's good food in the kitchen." She laughed and told him that he had been watching too many movies. But when she hung up, she kept thinking about what he had said.

Chapter 4: The Kitchen Is the Warmest Room

On the second Saturday without heat, Valeria came home early, because the library was closed for a holiday. The apartment was silent. She went into the kitchen, turned on the oven, opened its door and stood in front of it with her hands out.

A minute later, Mei came in. She was wearing two sweaters and a blanket around her shoulders. She looked at Valeria, then at the open oven, and laughed for the first time since Valeria had known her.

"I thought I was the only one who did that," Mei said.

"In Guadalajara, we don't even have heat," said Valeria. "We don't need it. I had no idea that a person could be this cold."

Mei pulled a chair up to the oven and sat down. "In Taichung, it is warm too. Hot, actually. My mother asked me on the phone yesterday why I had chosen to study in a freezer."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her that the architecture program here is one of the best in the world," Mei said. "And she said that the best program in the world is useless if I get sick." She sighed. "She was probably right."

It occurred to Valeria that if they were going to stand in front of the oven, they might as well cook something. She looked in her kitchen cabinet. She had rice, beans, an onion, some tomatoes and a bag of dried chilies that her aunt had hidden in her suitcase.

"Do you like spicy food?" she asked.

Mei's eyes lit up. "Do I like spicy food," she repeated, as if the question was a joke.

They were chopping onions together when Kwame came home. He stopped in the doorway and sniffed the air. "What is that?" he asked. "And can I help?"

He went to his room and came back with a bag of plantains and a jar of red pepper sauce. It had been made by his grandmother, he warned them, and it should be treated with respect. Twenty minutes later, Pieter appeared, attracted by the smell. He stood in the doorway for a while, looking unsure, until Mei handed him a knife and a pile of tomatoes.

It was the first time all four of them had been in the kitchen at the same time.

Chapter 5: The Author of the Notes

They ate at the small kitchen table, sitting so close that their elbows touched. The oven was still open, and the room was warm for the first time in two weeks. The food was very spicy, and Pieter's face turned slowly red, but he kept eating and insisted that he was fine.

Halfway through the meal, Kwame pointed at the fridge with his fork. "Can I ask something?" he said. "Who writes those notes?"

There was a silence. Valeria looked at Mei. Mei looked at Kwame. Then everybody looked at Pieter, who had put down his spoon.

"They're not mine," Pieter said quickly. Then he paused. "Well. The first one is mine. From last year. I wrote it for a guy who lived in room four before Valeria. He never washed anything. It was a disaster."

"And the others?" asked Mei.

Pieter looked confused. "I thought they were yours."

It turned out that nobody had written the other notes. Or rather, everybody admitted to one, eventually. Kwame confessed that he had written the note about the milk. He had been annoyed one morning, and he had copied Pieter's handwriting style "to make it look official." Mei admitted that the bathroom note was hers. She had seen the first two notes and had assumed that this was simply how things were done in the Netherlands.

"I thought it was a Dutch tradition," she said. "Like cheese. Or bicycles."

Pieter put his head in his hands. "It is not a Dutch tradition," he said. "I was just too shy to knock on that guy's door."

Valeria started laughing, and then she couldn't stop. For three months, they had all been living with a ghost that they had created together. If anyone had knocked on a single door, the whole mystery would have been solved in five minutes.

"So who used the milk?" she asked when she could breathe again.

Kwame raised his hand slowly. "That was also me," he said. "I was very tired."

Chapter 6: A Letter That Works

After dinner, nobody wanted to go back to their freezing rooms. They stayed in the kitchen and talked about the heat instead.

"We should have done something together from the start," said Kwame. "Four separate emails are easy to ignore. One letter from all of us is not."

Pieter knew that the city had an office that helped tenants with problems like this. He had learned about it when his sister had a similar problem in Utrecht. Tenants, the people who rent a home, could ask that office for help if a landlord ignored them. The heat, he said, definitely counted as a serious problem in December.

So they wrote a letter together. Mei typed, because she was the fastest. Pieter checked the details, because he understood the system. Kwame made sure the tone was polite but firm, because he said he had learned that from years of arguing with his older brothers. And Valeria made a list of every date. She noted when the heat had broken, when each email had been sent and what they had been told.

In the letter, they reminded Mr. Visser that they had first reported the problem eighteen days ago. They wrote that they had been promised a repair by Friday, and that the promise had not been kept. They said they were asking for the boiler to be repaired within three working days. If it was not, they would contact the city's office for tenants. They all signed it.

"If this doesn't work," said Pieter, "I don't know what will."

"It'll work," said Valeria. She wasn't sure, but she felt braver than she had in weeks.

They sent the letter at eleven o'clock that night.

Chapter 7: The Technician

The next morning, at eight o'clock, Valeria's phone rang. It was Mr. Visser. He sounded different. Less busy, and much more polite.

He said that he had read their letter and that he was very sorry for the delay. He explained that the part had been delivered to the wrong address and that nobody had told him. A technician would be at the apartment that afternoon, he said, and he would personally make sure the job was finished. He also said that a small discount would be given on next month's rent, to apologize for the inconvenience.

The technician arrived at two o'clock. He was an older man with a gray beard and very large hands. He spent an hour inside the boiler closet, talking quietly to himself. When he came out, he told them the boiler should have been replaced years ago. It would survive the winter now, he said, but only just.

"Who was living here last winter?" he asked, wiping his hands.

Pieter said that he had been.

The technician shook his head. "Then you must have been cold too," he said. "This thing has been dying slowly for a long time. Someone should have called me a year ago."

Pieter admitted that he had noticed the radiators getting weaker. He had meant to write a note about it.

At three thirty, the radiators began to make a soft knocking sound. By five, the apartment was warm. Valeria took off her coat, then her hat, then her second pair of socks. Kwame lay down on the living room floor, next to the radiator, and announced that he was never going to move again.

Chapter 8: New Notes

The heat has worked perfectly ever since. But something else changed in the apartment that winter, and it has not changed back.

The four of them now cook together every Sunday. Each week, someone different chooses the food. Pieter made a thick pea soup that, he said, had been eaten by Dutch families for centuries. Mei taught them to make dumplings. Kwame introduced them to jollof rice and started a long, serious argument about which country in West Africa made the best version. Valeria's chilies were used so often that her aunt had to send a second bag by mail.

And the notes are still on the fridge. But they are different now.

"Dumplings on Sunday. Bring an appetite."

"Kwame, you have a package downstairs. It is huge. What did you buy?"

"Good luck on your exam, Valeria! We know you'll do great."

"Thank you for cleaning the bathroom. It was noticed and it was appreciated. — P."

In March, when the days were finally getting longer, Valeria's mother called and asked her how she was getting along with her roommates. Valeria looked at the fridge, covered in colorful pieces of paper, each one signed with a name.

"At the beginning, I thought I had moved in with three strangers," she told her mother. "I was wrong. I had moved in with three friends. We just hadn't met yet."
