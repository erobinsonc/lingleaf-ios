Chapter 1: The Bank of the River

When Mirela Popescu was a girl, she spent every summer sitting on the bank of a wide brown river, watching other children swim.

The river ran past the edge of her village in the south of Romania, slow and warm and full of light. On hot afternoons, the whole village seemed to end up there. Boys jumped in from the old wooden jetty, shouting. Girls swam out to a sandbank in the middle, and they lay on their backs in the sun. Even the old men waded in up to their knees and stood there smoking and discussing the weather.

Mirela sat on the grass with her dress pulled over her knees and her shoes beside her. Her mother did not swim. Her mother had never swum in her life, and she was afraid of the river with a deep, quiet fear that she never explained. "It looks gentle," she used to say. "That's what makes it dangerous."

So Mirela was allowed to go to the river, but not into it. She could put her feet in the water at the edge. She could not go any further.

It was not a tragic childhood. It was a happy one, in most ways. But it is the things we were never allowed to do that stay with us longest, and the river stayed with Mirela for the rest of her life.

She was seventy-one now, and she had lived in Valencia, on the east coast of Spain, for almost thirty years. She had come for a job as a pharmacist's assistant, met a Spanish engineer named Vicente at a bus stop in the rain, married him and stayed. Vicente had died six years ago. Their daughter, Elisa, lived ten minutes away with her husband and their daughter, Ioana. Ioana was ten years old and had her grandmother's dark eyes and her grandfather's complete lack of patience.

For thirty years, Mirela had lived in a city by the sea, and for thirty years she had sat on the sand while her family swam. She had always had a reason. The water was too cold. She had just eaten. She was reading. She would go in later.

Vicente, who had swum like a fish, had stopped asking after the first few summers. He had simply brought her a cold drink and sat down beside her for a while, dripping, before going back in.

If he had pushed her, she sometimes thought, she might have learned. But he had not been that kind of man. And if he had been that kind of man, she would not have married him.

Chapter 2: Lane Four

It was Ioana who changed everything, although she had no idea she was doing it.

It happened on a Sunday in March, at lunch. Ioana had just finished her first term of swimming lessons at school, and she was full of it. She could do twenty-five meters of front crawl, she announced. She could dive to the bottom of the deep end and pick up a ring. Her teacher said she was a natural.

"In the summer," she said to her grandmother, between mouthfuls of chicken, "you have to swim out to the buoy with me. The yellow one. It's not far. Papa says it's only fifty meters."

There was a short silence at the table. Elisa looked at her mother, and then at her daughter, and opened her mouth to explain.

"Bunica can't swim," Mirela said, before Elisa could speak. "Bunica" was what Ioana called her, the Romanian word for grandmother. "I never learned."

Ioana stared at her with the honest astonishment of a child who has just been told that an adult cannot read. "But everyone can swim," she said.

"Not everyone."

"Then you should learn," said Ioana, as though it were the simplest thing in the world. "There's a pool near your apartment. I've seen it. They have classes for old people. It says so on the sign."

"Ioana!" said Elisa.

"It's fine," said Mirela. "She's right. They do."

She had walked past the sign a hundred times. It was attached to the fence of the municipal sports center, two streets from her apartment. "AQUATIC ACTIVITIES FOR OVER-65s," it said, in blue letters. "ALL LEVELS. BEGINNERS WELCOME."

She had read it the way one reads a sign for a dance class. She had felt mild interest, and absolute certainty that it was meant for somebody else.

That evening, she walked past the sign again. This time, she stopped. She stood there for a long time, until a man with a dog looked at her curiously. Then she went home and sat at the kitchen table with her hands around a cup of tea.

What frightened her, she realized, was not the water. It was the idea of being bad at something in front of other people. She had been competent for so long. She had run a pharmacy counter for twenty-five years, raised a daughter, buried a husband and learned a second language well enough to argue with the electricity company in it. She was not used to being a beginner.

The next morning, before she could change her mind, she went to the sports center and signed up.

The young man at the reception desk took her details without looking up from his screen. Name, address, date of birth. "Level?" he asked.

"I'm sorry?"

"Swimming level. Beginner, intermediate or advanced."

Mirela hesitated. She thought about saying "intermediate," just to see how it felt. Then she thought about the shallow end, and the other people in it, and what the instructor, whoever she turned out to be, would think when she saw her.

"Beginner," she said. "Complete beginner. I have never been in a swimming pool in my life."

The young man looked up for the first time. He studied her for a moment, and then he smiled, a real smile, not a polite one.

"My grandmother started last year," he said. "Now she won't stop talking about it. Tuesday and Thursday, ten o'clock. Lane four." He printed a small card with her name on it and handed it across the desk. "Bring a cap. They're strict about the caps."

Chapter 3: Face in the Water

The class met on Tuesday and Thursday mornings at ten, in the shallow end of the indoor pool, in lane four. The instructor was a young woman named Lucía Benítez, who could not have been more than twenty-six. She had short, sun-bleached hair, a whistle she never used and the calm, unhurried manner of someone who had spent a great deal of time teaching frightened people.

There were six students in the beginners' group. Two were sisters in their late sixties, from a village inland, who had grown up far from any water. One was a retired taxi driver named Paco, who said he had swum as a boy but had "forgotten how" and was clearly embarrassed about it. One was a tiny, cheerful woman of eighty-two named Remedios, who could in fact swim perfectly well but came, she said, "for the company and the gossip." And one was a tall, thin man with white hair and a neatly trimmed beard. He stood very straight at the edge of the pool and did not speak to anyone.

On the first day, Mirela did not get her hair wet. She walked down the steps into the shallow end, where the water came up to her waist. She stood there holding the edge, and she felt her heart beating in her throat. If she had learned as a girl, she thought, she wouldn't be standing here holding the edge like a frightened child.

It was absurd. The water was warm. The bottom of the pool was clearly visible. A lifeguard was sitting on a high chair three meters away, reading a newspaper. Nothing could possibly happen to her. And still she could not let go.

"You needn't do anything today," said Lucía, who had come to stand beside her. "Just stand. Get used to it. Feel how the water holds you up a little."

"It doesn't feel like it's holding me up," said Mirela. "It feels like it's waiting."

Lucía laughed, not unkindly. "That's a very good description," she said. "Most people feel that. It goes away."

It did not go away on the first day, or the second. On the third day, Lucía asked them to put their faces in the water and blow bubbles. The sisters did it at once, giggling like schoolgirls. Paco did it, came up coughing and did it again. Remedios did it with the elegance of a woman who had been swimming for seventy years.

Mirela bent toward the water until her chin touched the surface. Then she stopped. She could not make herself go any further. Her whole body refused, as firmly as her mother had once refused at the edge of the river.

"It's all right," said Lucía. "Try just your mouth. Then your nose. There's no hurry."

Mirela tried. She got her mouth in, and blew, and a few small bubbles came up. Then she lifted her head and wiped her face with both hands as though she had been underwater for a minute.

"That," said Lucía, "is exactly how it starts."

Mirela did not believe her. That evening, she nearly called the sports center to cancel. She sat with the phone in her hand for some time. In the end, it was not courage that stopped her. It was the thought of Ioana's face when she told her.

Elisa came over on Saturday with a bag of oranges and a worried expression. She had heard about the lessons from Ioana, who had told everyone, including the man at the bakery.

"Mamá, are you sure about this?" she asked, as soon as she sat down. "You don't have to prove anything. Nobody expects you to swim at seventy-one."

"That," said Mirela, "is exactly the problem."

Elisa looked at her for a long moment. "Is it safe? Your back, your knees..."

"There is a lifeguard who reads the newspaper, an instructor who never blows her whistle and a woman of eighty-two who swims better than both of us," said Mirela. "I think I will survive."

Elisa laughed, despite herself. "You're impossible," she said.

"I'm a beginner," said Mirela. "It's very different." She was surprised to hear how much she liked the sound of the word.

Chapter 4: Mr. Rahman's Towel

The tall man with the white beard was called Mr. Rahman. Mirela learned his name on the fourth Tuesday, in the café across the street from the sports center. Remedios had insisted that the whole class go there for coffee after the lesson.

He sat at the end of the table, with a cup of tea he did not drink, and answered Remedios's many questions politely and briefly. He was seventy-six. He had come to Valencia from Bangladesh more than forty years ago. He had worked as a tailor, first for other people and later in a small shop of his own near the old market. He had retired two years before. His wife had died in the winter.

"And you never learned to swim?" asked Remedios, who was not a woman to leave a question unasked. "In Bangladesh? I thought there was water everywhere."

"There is water everywhere," said Mr. Rahman. "That is why my parents did not want me to go near it." He smiled slightly, for the first time. "When there is a lot of water, people are often more careful of it, not less."

Mirela looked at him across the table, and something in her recognized something in him.

"My mother was the same," she said. "A river. She never let me in."

He turned and looked at her properly. "Then we are the same," he said. "Two people who grew up beside water, and never touched it."

After that, they began to arrive early, and to wait for each other at the edge of the pool. Mr. Rahman's difficulty was the opposite of hers. He could put his face in the water quite easily, and could even hold his breath for a long time. What he could not do was let his feet leave the bottom. The moment he felt himself begin to float, he grabbed for the edge as though the floor had disappeared.

"It must be a kind of instinct," he said one morning, standing in the shallow end with the water up to his chest. "My body thinks it knows better than my mind."

"Mine too," said Mirela. "Mine thinks it's still nine years old."

Remedios, meanwhile, had appointed herself the class's unofficial grandmother, although she was barely older than some of them. She brought homemade cookies to the café on Thursdays and knew everybody's business within a week. She knew that Paco's wife had signed him up for the lessons without asking him. She knew that the sisters had never been to the sea, although they lived only an hour away. It was Remedios, too, who first noticed that Mirela and Mr. Rahman always sat next to each other.

"Very nice," she said to Mirela one morning in the changing room, with a meaningful look.

"We are both beginners," said Mirela firmly. "That's all."

"Of course," said Remedios. "Beginners. That's how it always starts." She went off to the pool humming, and Mirela realized, to her own great surprise, that she was blushing.

He was a precise, orderly man. He folded his towel into a perfect square on the bench before every lesson and placed his glasses on top of it, exactly in the center. One morning, he arrived to find that the bench had been moved and his usual place was gone. Mirela watched him stand there, holding the towel, looking quietly lost. Then he unfolded it, folded it again and placed it on the next bench along, with the glasses exactly in the center.

She did not know why that moment moved her so much. Perhaps it was because she understood it. When the large thing in your life is gone, you hold on to the small ones.

"May I ask you something?" she said to him in the café that day. "Why did you start? The lessons. You could have simply stayed at home."

He was quiet for a long time.

"My wife always wanted to go to the sea," he said at last. "She grew up in the north, far from the coast. Every summer she asked me to take her, and every summer I found a reason not to. The shop, the money, the heat." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Had I known how few summers we had, I would have taken her every year. Now I cannot take her. But I could at least go into the sea myself." He looked up. "It seems foolish, perhaps."

"It doesn't seem foolish at all," said Mirela.

Chapter 5: Floating

It happened on a Thursday in the middle of May, and it happened, as the important things often do, almost by accident.

Lucía had brought out long foam floats, bright orange and blue, and had asked everyone to hold one under their arms and lean back. Mirela had done this many times by now. She could lie back on the float with her face to the ceiling, her feet barely touching the bottom, and feel, almost, that it was pleasant. She had begun to put her whole face in the water. She had even, once, opened her eyes underneath and seen the blue lines on the floor of the pool, wavering like a dream.

But she had never let go of the float.

That Thursday, she was lying back and looking at the high windows above the pool, where the morning sun was coming through in long, pale bars. Remedios was talking to the sisters at the far end. Paco was attempting something that might, with great generosity, have been called a backstroke. And Mirela was thinking, for no particular reason, about the river. She was thinking about the sandbank and the girls lying on their backs on it. She had always wondered how it would feel to lie in the sun with water all around you.

She didn't decide to let go of the float. She simply noticed, after a while, that it had drifted away from her, and that she was still lying on the water.

For a moment, she was perfectly still. The water held her. It held her shoulders and the back of her head and the backs of her legs, firmly and gently, the way a hand holds something it doesn't want to break. She could hear her own breathing, very loud, and beyond it the muffled sounds of the pool.

"Lucía," she said, quite calmly. "Lucía. I'm floating."

Lucía turned. Her face, which was usually so calm, broke into a huge smile. "You are," she said. "Don't think about it. Just breathe."

Mirela breathed. She lay there on the surface of the water for perhaps fifteen seconds, which was the longest fifteen seconds of her life. Then she thought about it, and sank, and stood up spluttering in water that came up to her waist. But it didn't matter.

Across the lane, someone was clapping. It was Mr. Rahman. He was standing in the shallow end, holding the edge of the pool with one hand and clapping against his chest with the other, and he was laughing.

"Now me," he said. "If you can do it, I have no excuse."

After the class, Mirela stayed behind to thank Lucía. The pool was empty now, and the water was settling into a flat, bright sheet.

"Can I ask why you teach this class?" said Mirela. "You must have offers to teach children, or athletes. Young people who learn fast."

Lucía was coiling the lane rope. She did not answer at once. "My grandfather couldn't swim," she said eventually. "He was a fisherman, near Alicante, and he couldn't swim a single stroke. Lots of them couldn't, in those days. He used to say that if you could swim, the sea would only take longer to drown you." She smiled. "When he was seventy-four, I taught him. In this pool, actually. It took a whole summer."

"Did he enjoy it?"

"He said it was the best summer of his life," said Lucía. "He swam every morning until he was eighty-nine. I should have charged him." She laughed, and then she looked at Mirela more seriously. "Children learn fast, but they don't remember learning. People in this class remember every single second. That's why I teach it."

It took him three more weeks. But on the second Tuesday in June, it finally happened. Mirela stood on one side of him and Lucía on the other. Mr. Rahman let his feet leave the bottom of the pool, and he lay back on the water for nine whole seconds. When he stood up again, he was breathing hard, and his glasses were on the bench, and he was not quite able to speak. He didn't need to.

Chapter 6: The Sea in August

At the last lesson of the term, Lucía gave each of them a small paper certificate with their name on it and a drawing of a fish. The sisters laughed at theirs. Paco folded his very carefully and put it in his wallet. Mr. Rahman studied his for a long time, as though he were checking the stitching on a jacket.

"I have many certificates," he said to Mirela in the café afterward. "From the tailoring school, from the city, from the association of shopkeepers. This one, I think, I will put on the wall."

By the end of July, Mirela could swim, slowly and without much style, the full twenty-five meters of the pool. She swam a kind of careful breaststroke with her head held high, like a swan that was slightly worried about its hair. Lucía said it was perfect. Remedios said it was "very dignified." Paco said nothing, because he had finally remembered how to swim and was too busy doing it.

She did not tell Ioana. She told Elisa, and made her promise to keep it a secret. Elisa cried a little on the phone and then said, "Mamá, you could have told me you wanted to learn. I would have paid for lessons years ago."

"I know," said Mirela. "But I wouldn't have gone. It had to be my own idea."

"It was Ioana's idea."

"Then it had to be Ioana's idea," said Mirela. "Some ideas sound better from a ten-year-old."

On the second Sunday in August, the whole family went to the beach at seven in the morning, before the heat and the crowds. The sea was flat and silver, and the yellow buoy was floating where it always was, about fifty meters out. Elisa and her husband put down the umbrella. Ioana ran straight to the water, as she always did, and then turned around, as she always did, to wave at her grandmother on the sand.

Mirela was not on the sand.

She was walking down to the water in a navy-blue swimsuit she had bought for the occasion, with a white swimming cap pulled firmly over her hair. Ioana stared at her. Then she put both hands over her mouth.

"Bunica," she said. "Bunica, are you going in?"

"I believe I am," said Mirela.

The sea was colder than the pool and much less polite. Small waves slapped against her legs and her stomach, and the sand moved under her feet. For a moment, the old fear came back, the feeling that the water was waiting. She stood there with the sea up to her chest, breathing. Then she leaned forward, the way Lucía had taught her, and let the water take her weight, and began to swim.

It was a very slow fifty meters. Ioana swam beside her the whole way, chattering and laughing and occasionally swallowing seawater, and twice Mirela had to stop and float on her back to rest. Each time she did, she looked up at the enormous pale sky, and she felt the sea holding her. She thought of the girls on the sandbank in the middle of the river, sixty years ago. If her mother had been able to see her now, she thought, she would have been terrified. And then, perhaps, a little proud.

When they reached the buoy, Ioana grabbed it with both hands and shouted something to her parents on the beach that nobody could hear. Mirela held on to it beside her, breathing hard, her heart racing in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

"You did it," said Ioana. "You actually did it."

"We did it," said Mirela.

Back on the beach, as she was drying herself with a towel, she noticed a tall, thin figure. He was standing at the edge of the water, a little further along the sand. He was wearing dark green swimming shorts and holding a neatly folded towel, and he was looking out at the sea with great concentration.

She wrapped her towel around her shoulders and walked along the beach toward him.

"Mr. Rahman," she said.

He turned, and his face lit up. "Mrs. Popescu. I saw you swim to the buoy. It was magnificent."

"It was very slow."

"Slow is a kind of magnificent, at our age," he said. Then he looked down at the towel in his hands. "This is the first time I have been in the sea. I have been standing here for twenty minutes. My wife," he added, quietly, "would have been in the water already. She would have been laughing at me."

Mirela didn't say anything for a moment. Then she held out her hand.

"Come on," she said. "I'll go in with you. It's colder than the pool, and it moves. But it holds you up all the same."

He looked at her hand, and then at the sea. Then he placed his towel very carefully on the sand, folded into a perfect square, and put his glasses exactly in the center.

They walked into the water together, slowly, the way you walk into a room where something important is about to happen. Behind them, on the beach, Ioana was explaining to her parents very loudly that Bunica had a friend from swimming class. He was a tailor, she said, and they were both very brave.
