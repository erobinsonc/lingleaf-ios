Chapter 1: Form 14-B

The form was called Exit Interview (Voluntary Departures), and it was four pages long, and Peter Kariuki had never seen it before in his life.

He sat at his desk on the sixth floor of the Kilima Freight building, on the edge of Nairobi's industrial area, and studied it the way he might have studied the instructions for an unfamiliar piece of machinery. Outside his window, trucks were moving slowly through the yard in the late-afternoon heat, their drivers shouting to one another over the noise of engines. Beyond the yard, the city stretched away into a haze of dust and light.

"Section one," he read aloud. "Reasons for leaving. Section two: Satisfaction with management. Section three: What could the company have done differently?" He put the form down. "Who wrote this?"

"Human Resources," said Halima Owino, who was standing in his doorway with her laptop under her arm and her car keys already in her hand. Halima was the head of HR, and she was leaving that evening for three weeks of long-planned leave. Her daughter was getting married in Kisumu. "It's company policy. Every voluntary departure gets an exit interview. Normally I'd do it myself, but Njeri's last day is Friday and I'll be at a wedding. Policy requires that the interview be conducted by a senior manager. That means you."

"I'm her manager. Isn't that a conflict of interest?"

"It's a conversation, Peter, not a court case." Halima smiled at him with the patience of someone who had spent twenty years persuading senior managers to do things they did not want to do. "Just ask the questions. Write down what she says. Don't argue with her. Don't try to change her mind. The form recommends that the interviewer remain neutral at all times." She tapped the page. "It's on page one. In bold."

"Neutral," Peter repeated.

"Neutral," said Halima. "You know. Like Switzerland." And she was gone, down the corridor and into the elevator, before he could think of a reply.

He looked at the form for a long time after she had gone. Then he looked through the glass wall of his office at the open-plan floor beyond it, where twenty-three people sat at long white desks under the humming lights. Most of them were packing up for the day. One of them was not.

Njeri Kamau sat at the far end of the room, by the window, with her headphones on and her eyes fixed on two screens full of numbers. She was twenty-nine. She had joined the company five years ago as a junior analyst, straight from a master's program in operations research, and in that time she had done something that nobody else in Kilima Freight's thirty-year history had managed to do. She had rebuilt, almost single-handedly, the system that decided which truck went where.

It was a dull-sounding thing, routing. It was also the heart of the business.

Before Njeri, the company's trucks had been scheduled by a combination of old software, older spreadsheets and the memory of a dispatcher named Mr. Kiprono, who had retired two years ago and taken half the company's knowledge with him. After Njeri, the trucks drove eleven percent fewer empty kilometers. Fuel costs had fallen. Deliveries were faster. Customers had stopped calling Peter to complain. If Njeri were to leave, Peter had once thought, half the system would leave with her.

He remembered the day he had hired her. She had come to the interview in a borrowed jacket that was slightly too big for her, carrying a printed report that nobody had asked for. It was a twelve-page analysis of Kilima Freight's delivery times, built entirely from public data, with three suggestions at the end. Two of them had been naive. The third had been so obviously right that Peter had felt slightly embarrassed that nobody in the company had thought of it.

"Why did you do this?" he had asked her, holding up the report.

"Because I wanted you to know what I could do," she had said, "before you decided whether to hire me." She had paused, and then added, with the directness he would later come to know very well: "And because I didn't want to work somewhere that wasn't interested in being told it was wrong."

He had hired her the same afternoon. It was, he sometimes thought, the fastest decision he had made in a decade.

And on Monday morning, without any warning at all, she had walked into his office and handed him a letter of resignation.

Chapter 2: Neutral

He had not asked her why. That, he would admit to himself later, was the first mistake.

She had put the letter on his desk, very neatly, as if it were a report. She had said that she had accepted a position with another company, that she would work her full notice period and that she would make sure everything was properly handed over. She had been calm, polite and entirely unreadable. He had said, "I see," and then, "Well, congratulations," and then, "We'll miss you," all of which were true, and none of which were what he had wanted to say.

What he had wanted to say was: "Why didn't you tell me you were unhappy?" He had not said it. He had been afraid of the answer, and it was easier, as it always was, to say nothing and let the moment pass.

It was a habit, saying nothing. He had developed it over many years, and it had served him well, or so he had believed. Never commit too early. Never decide what can be postponed. Never shut a door you might need to walk through later.

He had been operations director for eight years, and in those eight years he had made very few decisions that anyone could later blame him for. He considered this a strength. He was beginning, that week, to wonder whether it was.

He scheduled the exit interview for Thursday afternoon, in the small meeting room at the end of the corridor, the one with the broken blind and the view of the water tank. He sent Njeri a calendar invitation. She accepted it within thirty seconds, without comment.

On Wednesday night, at home, he read the form again at the kitchen table while his wife, Wairimu, graded her students' essays on the other side. Wairimu taught history at a high school, and she graded essays the way other people did crosswords: fast, ruthlessly and with obvious enjoyment.

"You've read that four times," she said, without looking up.

"It's a difficult form."

"It's four pages long, and you're a director." She wrote something in the margin of an essay in red ink and turned the page. "What's really difficult about it?"

Peter was silent for a moment. "Section two," he said. "Satisfaction with management."

Wairimu looked up then. She had known him for twenty-six years, and she was rarely wrong about what his silences meant.

"You think she's leaving because of you."

"I don't know why she's leaving. I didn't ask."

"Why not?"

"Because Halima says I have to be neutral."

"Peter." Wairimu put down her pen. "You didn't ask because you didn't want to know. Halima had nothing to do with it. In your head, Halima was in Kisumu before she even got into the elevator."

He wanted to argue. He found that he could not.

"If I were you," Wairimu said, more gently, "I'd ask her the questions on that form, and then I'd stop talking and listen. Properly. Not the way you listen in meetings, when you're already planning how to avoid deciding anything."

"I don't do that."

Wairimu raised her eyebrows and returned to her essays. Peter returned to his form. Neither of them said anything for some time.

Chapter 3: Section One

On Thursday morning he arrived at the office an hour early, which he never did, and sat in the parking lot with the engine off, watching the first trucks pull out of the gate. He had brought the form with him. He had also, for reasons he could not quite explain, brought a notebook, a real paper one, of the kind he had used as a junior clerk thirty years earlier, before everything moved onto screens.

On the first page, he had written three words in capital letters: LISTEN. DON'T DEFEND. Underneath, after thinking it over, he had added a fourth, smaller: ASK.

It seemed absurd, a man of fifty-four writing himself instructions like a schoolboy before an examination. But then, he thought, perhaps that was what this was. He had been grading other people's work for a long time. It had been a very long time since anybody had graded his.

The meeting room was too warm, as it always was in the afternoons, and the broken blind let in a single bright bar of sunlight, which stretched across the table between them like a line on a contract. Njeri sat opposite him with a glass of water and her hands folded in front of her. She had dressed, he noticed, slightly more formally than usual, as if for an interview of another kind.

"Thank you for doing this," Peter said. "I know it's a formality."

"It's fine," said Njeri. "I'd rather do it than not."

He picked up the form. His hands, he was annoyed to discover, were not quite steady.

"Section one," he read. "Reasons for leaving. Could you tell me, in your own words, why you've decided to leave the company?"

She looked at him for a moment, as though deciding how much to say.

"I've been offered a role as head of analytics at a smaller firm," she said. "In Mombasa. They move fruit and vegetables to the port. It's a better title, and more money, though not much more."

He wrote it down. "Better title. More money." It was, he thought, almost a relief. Title and money were ordinary reasons. Nobody could be blamed for those.

"Is that the main reason?" he asked, because the form told him to.

There was a pause.

"No," said Njeri. "It isn't."

He waited. The bar of sunlight had moved a few centimeters along the table. Somewhere outside, a truck was reversing, beeping steadily.

"The main reason," she said, "is that in Mombasa, I'll be allowed to make decisions."

Peter kept his pen on the paper. He did not write anything.

"Can you say a bit more about that?" he asked.

"I can," she said. "Do you want me to?"

It was a fair question, and it deserved an honest answer, and Peter sat for a long moment trying to find one. He thought of Wairimu at the kitchen table, raising her eyebrows. He thought of the word "neutral", in bold, on page one.

"Yes," he said. "I do."

Chapter 4: Section Two

What she told him over the next forty minutes, he would remember almost word for word for the rest of his working life.

She did not raise her voice. She did not accuse him of anything. She spoke the way she wrote her reports: clearly, in order, with evidence. That was, in a way, the hardest part.

Had she been angry, he could have defended himself. Had she exaggerated, he could have corrected her. She did neither.

"Two years ago," she said, "I proposed that we stop running the Eldoret route on Tuesdays and combine it with Thursday. The trucks were going out half empty. You said it was a good idea and that you'd take it to the board. Do you remember what happened?"

"The board wanted more data."

"The board didn't see it," said Njeri. "I asked Halima. It wasn't on the agenda. It wasn't on any agenda that year. We ran half-empty trucks to Eldoret for fourteen more months. Then Mr. Ochieng from finance suggested exactly the same thing, at a meeting I wasn't invited to, and it was approved in ten minutes."

Peter opened his mouth. He closed it again.

"Last year," she went on, "I built a model that showed we could cut the night shift in the yard from three supervisors to two without any loss of safety. I spent four months on it. You said you agreed. Then you asked me to do a second version, with different assumptions. Then a third. Then you said it might be better to wait until after the elections. Then until after the new financial year. It's still on the shared drive. Version seven."

Peter remembered each of those versions. He remembered asking for them, and he remembered, each time, the small, warm feeling of relief as the decision moved a few months further into the future. He had thought of it as caution. He had thought of it, if he was honest, as wisdom: the patience of an experienced man who did not rush. Hearing it described now, in her calm voice, across a table, it sounded like something else entirely. It sounded like a man standing in a doorway for two years, unable to go in or out.

"There were concerns about the union," Peter said, and immediately wished he had not.

"There were," said Njeri calmly. "I spoke to the union representative myself, in March. She was fine with it. She said she'd been waiting for someone to ask her for two years. I sent you an email about it. You didn't reply."

He remembered the email. He remembered reading it, and thinking, "I'll deal with that next week," and closing it. He had not thought about it since.

"It isn't that you said no," Njeri said. "I could have lived with no. People say no to my ideas all the time; that's normal; that's how it's supposed to work. It's that you never said anything. Every idea I've had in five years has gone into your office and disappeared, like a truck going into a tunnel that doesn't come out the other side. Some of them came out again years later with somebody else's name on them. Most of them didn't come out at all."

The bar of sunlight had reached the edge of the table now. It lay across the back of his hand.

"I don't think you do it on purpose," she said. "I think you do it because you're afraid of being wrong. And I understand that. Really, I do. But in Mombasa, if I'm wrong, I'll find out in a month, and I'll fix it. Here, I never find out anything. I just wait." She paused, and for the first time her composure flickered. "I'm twenty-nine. I don't want to spend my thirties waiting."

Peter looked down at the form. Under "Section two: Satisfaction with management," there was a scale from one to five and a small box for comments. The box was about three centimeters high.

"I'm not sure," he said slowly, "that there's room on the form for all of that."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face. "No," she said. "I didn't think there would be."

Chapter 5: Section Three

He should, according to the form, have moved on to section three. Instead, he put the pen down.

"Can I tell you something?" he asked. "It isn't on the form. You can say no."

Njeri considered him. "Go on."

"Ten years ago, before I was director, I made a decision." He looked at the water tank through the broken blind. "A big one. We had the chance to bid for a contract with a mining company in the north. Huge volumes. I pushed hard for it. I convinced the board. We bought eight new trucks on the strength of it." He stopped. "Six months later, the mine closed. Nobody had seen it coming. We ended up with eight trucks, a lot of debt and no contract. Three people lost their jobs. Good people. One of them was my best friend from university."

Njeri said nothing. She was listening, he realized, in exactly the way that Wairimu had told him to listen: completely, without preparing a reply.

"After that," Peter said, "I promised myself I would never be the reason anyone lost their job again. And the safest way to make sure of that, I thought, was never to decide anything that could go wrong." He laughed, without much humor. "Which, it turns out, is everything."

"What happened to your friend?" Njeri asked.

"He's fine now. Better than fine. He runs his own transport company in Nakuru, with eleven trucks. Two of them are the ones we sold off after the contract collapsed." Peter smiled faintly. "He bought them at auction. He sends me a photograph every December, of himself standing in front of them. I've never been sure if it's a joke or a reproach."

"Probably both," said Njeri.

"Probably," said Peter. "He's never said I was wrong to bid for the contract. Not once. He says it was a reasonable decision that turned out badly, which is a different thing from a bad decision." He was quiet for a moment. "I've never quite believed him."

"I'm sorry," Njeri said quietly. "I didn't know."

"Why would you? I've never told anyone at work." He picked up the pen again and turned it over in his fingers. "I'm not telling you so that you'll feel sorry for me. I'm telling you because you were right, and I'd rather you knew why. You deserve the real reason. You've given me yours."

For a while, neither of them said anything. Outside, the truck had stopped reversing. The yard had gone quiet in the way it always did at around four o'clock, when the afternoon shift was out on the roads and the evening shift had not yet arrived.

"Section three," Peter said at last, reading from the page. "What could the company have done differently to retain you?"

Njeri looked at the form, and then at him.

"Honestly?" she said. "Two years ago, it could have said yes, or no, to the Eldoret route. Either one. Last year, it could have said yes, or no, to the night shift. If the company had made even one of those decisions, I'd probably still be here. I wouldn't be planning to move my whole life to a city where I don't know anybody."

He wrote it down, carefully, in the three-centimeter box. "Could have said yes, or no. Either one."

"And now?" he asked. "Is there anything that would change your mind?"

It was not a question on the form. Halima would not have approved. He knew it as soon as he had asked.

"No," said Njeri, gently but without hesitation. "I've signed the contract. I've found a flat. My mother has already told all her friends that her daughter is going to be a head of something." She smiled, a little sadly. "But thank you for asking. It's the first time you've asked me anything you didn't already know the answer to."

Chapter 6: Fourteen Months, Version Seven

He did not sleep much that night.

He lay awake beside Wairimu, listening to the dogs in the neighborhood barking at nothing, and thinking about trucks going into tunnels. At three in the morning he got up, made himself a cup of tea he did not drink and opened his laptop at the kitchen table. He found the night-shift model on the shared drive. Version seven. It was eighty-four pages long, with an executive summary of exactly one page, a safety analysis, a cost analysis and, at the end, a short appendix titled "Notes from discussion with union representative, 14 March."

He read all eighty-four pages. It took him until dawn.

It was good. More than good: it was careful, honest and complete. It anticipated every objection he could think of and several he could not. It had been sitting on the shared drive, untouched, for seven months. And it had been sitting there, he understood with a slow, cold clarity, not because there was anything wrong with it, but because approving it would have meant that if something went wrong, it would be his name on the decision.

At half past five, as the sky was turning gray, he did something else he had not done in years. He got into his car and drove to the yard.

The night shift was finishing. Under the tall white floodlights, the last trucks of the night were coming in, dusty and tired, and the morning trucks were being loaded for the day. In the small office by the gate, the senior night supervisor, a broad, gray-haired woman named Mrs. Nduta, was drinking tea from a flask and filling in the shift log by hand. She looked up in astonishment when the operations director walked in.

"Mr. Kariuki," she said. "Is something wrong?"

"No," said Peter. "I wanted to ask you something. About the night shift. About whether it could run with two supervisors instead of three."

Mrs. Nduta looked at him over the rim of her cup for a long time.

"The young woman's proposal," she said. "The analyst. Kamau."

"You know about it?"

"Everyone on nights knows about it," said Mrs. Nduta. "She came down here four, five times. Sat with us all night, twice. Asked questions until two in the morning. She knows more about this shift than some of the people who work it." She put her cup down. "Yes, it could run with two. Done the way she wrote it, with the new lighting on the north side, it would run better with two. I told her that in March. I told the union. We've been waiting since then for somebody upstairs to say yes."

"I'm sorry," said Peter, and he was surprised by how easily the words came.

"Don't be sorry," said Mrs. Nduta. "Be quick." She picked up her pen and went back to the log, and Peter understood that the conversation was over.

He drove home through the waking city, past women setting up fruit stalls at the roadside and long lines of people waiting for buses, and he thought about the phrase "somebody upstairs". He had always imagined, vaguely, that the people upstairs were the board. It had not occurred to him until that morning that, to Mrs. Nduta, the person upstairs was him.

Wairimu found him at the table at half past six, in the same clothes, with the laptop still open and the cold tea beside it.

"Well?" she said.

"She was right," said Peter. "About all of it."

Wairimu sat down opposite him. She did not say "I told you so," which he appreciated, though he could see it in her face.

"So what are you going to do?"

"I can't keep her," said Peter. "She's going. She should go. She'll be brilliant in Mombasa." He rubbed his eyes. "But I can stop doing it to the next one."

"And how will you do that?"

He looked at the laptop. Then he did something he had not done in a very long time, which was to answer a question about the future without first leaving himself a way out.

"I'm going to take the night-shift proposal to the board on Monday," he said. "Version seven. With her name on the front. And I'm going to recommend that they approve it."

"And if they don't?"

"Then they don't," said Peter. "And I'll know. And so will she. Even if they were to reject it, it would be the first honest answer she has had from this company in two years."

Wairimu looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached across the table and closed his laptop.

"Go and have a shower," she said. "You look like a man who has spent the night reading an appendix."

"I have."

"Then go and wash it off."

In the shower, with the water running over his face, he found himself thinking not about the board but about his friend in Nakuru, and the photographs every December. For years he had looked at them and seen an accusation. That morning, for the first time, he wondered whether they had simply been an invitation: "look, it went wrong, and here I am anyway."

Chapter 7: The Last Day

Friday was Njeri's last day.

There was a small gathering at four o'clock on the open-plan floor, with samosas from the cafeteria, a cake with her name piped on it in green icing and a card that had been circulating secretly around the building all week. It was signed by nearly everyone, including several of the drivers, one of whom had simply written, "Thank you for the shorter routes. My children see me more." Njeri read that one twice. Peter, who was standing at the edge of the group with a paper plate in his hand, noticed her swallow hard before she turned the page.

He had prepared a speech. He had written it out on the back of an old delivery schedule the night before. When the moment came, he looked at it, folded it up and put it in his pocket.

"I'm supposed to say a few words," he said. The room went quiet. "Most of you know that Njeri rebuilt our routing system. What some of you may not know is how many other things she built that never got past my desk."

There was a slight stir in the room. People glanced at one another. A manager speaking about his own failures in public was not something that happened often at Kilima Freight.

"On Monday," Peter went on, "I'll be taking one of them to the board: a proposal for the night shift in the yard that she finished seven months ago and that I should have taken to them seven months ago. It has her name on it. If it's approved, it will be because of her work. If it isn't, it will be because I waited too long." He paused. "I've also asked the IT team to rename the routing system. It will be called the Kamau Model from now on, in every document we produce. That's the least it deserves."

He raised his paper cup of orange juice, slightly awkwardly. "To Njeri. Mombasa is very lucky."

"To Njeri," said everyone, and there was applause, and then laughter, because one of the drivers had started singing, and somebody cut the cake.

Later, as the party was breaking up, Njeri found him by the window, looking down at the yard, where the evening shift was arriving and the first trucks were lining up at the gate.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"I did," said Peter. "Rather late, but I did."

She stood beside him for a moment, watching the trucks.

"The Kamau Model," she said, and he could not tell whether she was amused or moved; probably both. "My mother is going to be impossible."

"Mothers usually are." He hesitated. "Can I ask you for something? Not as your manager. You won't be my analyst anymore, after today."

"What?"

"If, in a year or two, you were to hear that I've gone back to doing what I did to you," he said, "I'd like you to tell me. Send me an email. Call me. Whatever you prefer. I'd rather hear it from you than not hear it at all."

Njeri looked at him for a long time. Then she held out her hand, and he shook it.

"Deal," she said. "But I'll expect a reply within a week."

"Within a day," said Peter.

"We'll see," said Njeri, and smiled, and went to collect her things.

On Monday morning, Peter took version seven to the board. He had prepared for the meeting differently from the way he usually prepared. Usually, he went into the boardroom with three possible positions, intending to choose among them only once he had seen which way the room was leaning. This time he went in with one. He had printed the summary page of version seven and placed a copy in front of each chair before anyone arrived, with Njeri's name in the top corner.

The chairwoman, a precise, elegant woman named Mrs. Odhiambo, had built the company's first warehouse with her husband thirty years earlier. She picked up the page and studied it before she sat down. Then she looked at Peter over her glasses.

"This is dated March," she said.

"It is," said Peter.

"Why are we only seeing it now?"

There it was: the question he had been dreading all weekend. He could have said there had been concerns about the union. He could have said that the timing had not been right. Were he to reach for one of them now, he knew, nobody in the room would question it. Several smooth, reasonable answers were available, and he had used every one of them at some point in the last eight years.

"Because I didn't bring it to you," he said. "That was my mistake, not hers."

Mrs. Odhiambo held his gaze for a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and turned to the first page.

They argued about it for two hours. Mr. Ochieng from finance asked for more data, and Peter, to his own considerable surprise, heard himself say that the data was already there, on page forty-one, and that he would rather they decided today than asked for an eighth version. There was a short, startled silence. Then the chairwoman suggested that the board vote.

It passed, five votes to two.

That evening, Peter opened his email and wrote a message to an address in Mombasa. It was very short. "Approved, 5–2. Your name on the front. Section three, completed. P."

He sent it before he could reread it, which for him was new.

The reply came eleven minutes later, while he was still sitting at his desk watching the yard empty for the night.

"Thank you. Genuinely. I've forwarded it to my mother, which you will regret, because she now insists that you come to dinner the next time you are in Mombasa." There was a line break, and then: "Mrs. Nduta called me this afternoon. She says you came to the yard at five in the morning and apologized to her. She says she nearly dropped her flask. Is it true?"

Peter looked at the message for a while. Then he typed: "Yes. Please tell your mother that I accept."

"Good," came the answer, almost at once. "She's already planning the menu."

He laughed aloud, alone in his office, loudly enough that the cleaner in the corridor put her head around the door to see whether he was all right. Then he took Form 14-B out of his briefcase, the one with the three-centimeter box full of his own handwriting, and put it in the folder marked HR, for Halima to find when she came back from the wedding.

At the bottom of the last page, under "Additional comments from interviewer," there was a line that the form did not require. He had written it on Thursday night, at the kitchen table, and he had not crossed it out.

"Interviewer was not neutral. Interviewer has learned something. Recommends that future interviewers not be neutral either."
